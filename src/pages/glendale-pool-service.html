Glendale: one city, three sources of tap water

The City of Glendale lists three sources for its drinking water. Salt River Project water is snowmelt and rain runoff from the Salt and Verde river watersheds. Central Arizona Project water is runoff from the Colorado River watershed, carried across the desert by canal. Groundwater is pumped from city wells.

Those supplies reach homes through more than one treatment plant. One of them, Pyramid Peak, sits in north Phoenix and is shared with Peoria: Glendale owns 77 percent of it and runs it, and it serves the northern parts of both cities. In a June 2025 water resources update, the city put its yearly CAP supply at 22,582 acre-feet, delivered by canal to Pyramid Peak and usable anywhere in the city's service area.

What Glendale's hardness numbers mean for a pool

A 2023 City of Phoenix report for a small system that Glendale supplied and operated put City of Glendale drinking water at 153 to 309 parts per million of hardness, roughly 9 to 18 grains per gallon, with an average of 229. The report notes that hardness is not a health standard. For a pool, though, the spread matters.

The top-off water varies

With several sources and plants in the system, the hardness of the water at your hose can be near the low end of that range or near the top. Two Glendale pools a few miles apart can drift at different speeds.

Evaporation leaves minerals behind

Water leaves the pool as vapor, but calcium does not. Each top-off adds more, so calcium hardness (the amount of dissolved calcium in the water) creeps up over the months, even when the water looks clear.

Scale shows up in two places

You can see it on tile and the waterline. You cannot see it inside a heater or on salt cell plates, where it cuts efficiency. Ask the pool company to test calcium hardness, not just chlorine and pH.

Safe checks before you call

  • Baskets. Empty the skimmer basket and, with the pump off, the pump basket. A packed basket starves the pump.
  • Water level. The water should reach about the middle of the skimmer opening. Too low and the pump can suck air.
  • Filter gauge. Note the pressure reading and tell the company. A reading well above normal points to a dirty filter.
  • Timer. Check that the pump schedule did not reset after a power blip.
  • Breaker. Reset a tripped pool breaker or GFCI once. If it trips again, leave it off and call.

Chemicals and children. Never mix pool chemicals with each other. Store them apart, dry and shaded, and out of children's reach, and follow the label. Keep children away from a pool that is not fenced or covered, and treat green or cloudy water as a drowning hazard, because no one can see the bottom.
