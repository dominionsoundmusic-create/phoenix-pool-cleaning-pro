Buckeye: a city that pumps all of its own water

The City of Buckeye's water utility relies almost entirely on groundwater for the water it delivers. The city reports roughly 11,970 acre-feet a year of drinking water, about 3.9 billion gallons, coming from 33 active wells. Much of Buckeye's planning area sits over the Hassayampa sub-basin, west of the White Tank Mountains. The city also holds a Colorado River allocation, and it recharges water into the aquifer.

Not every Buckeye home is on city water. The city says most residents are served by its own Water Resources Department, and two other water providers serve parts of the city. Which one serves you depends on the address, and the name is on your water bill. That matters for a pool, because each provider publishes its own water quality report and its own rules.

What Buckeye's groundwater does to a pool

The city says the minerals that occur naturally in its well water, typically calcium, sodium and magnesium, are not pleasing to customers for smell, taste and hardness. A pool feels that load more than a kitchen tap does.

Total dissolved solids

Total dissolved solids, or TDS, is the total amount of minerals and salts dissolved in water. The EPA treats it as an aesthetic matter with only a secondary guideline, not a health limit, and in a pool, TDS only climbs as water evaporates.

Calcium and scale

Calcium hardness is the amount of dissolved calcium in the water. When it runs high, it can come out of solution as white or gray scale on tile, in heaters and inside salt cells, which is why it is worth testing in the pool itself.

Wells chosen with care

The city says much of the local groundwater is poor quality and would be costly to treat, so it has worked to place its wells in areas with better water. That keeps tap water acceptable, but it does not make it soft.

A tight water supply and your pool

Water planning is a live issue in Buckeye. In January 2023 the Arizona Department of Water Resources released a groundwater model for the Lower Hassayampa sub-basin, and the city described the result as about 15 percent short of the water needed to meet Arizona's 100-year assured water supply test. Buckeye says it has the water to serve existing customers and the growth that already holds certificates of assured water supply, and it has been adding sources. In 2023 city leaders approved spending about $80 million on groundwater rights from outside the city, about 5,926 acre-feet a year.

The city's water portfolio also relies on the Central Arizona Groundwater Replenishment District, a state program that uses member fees to put water back into the ground to make up for groundwater pumped by its members. None of this limits normal pool use, but it is a good reason not to waste water. A slow leak, a filler valve that sticks or a drain that was not needed all waste groundwater.

Safe checks before you call

  • Waterline. Look for white or gray crust on the tile and note how far the water has dropped at the skimmer.
  • Baskets. Empty the skimmer and pump baskets, which fill quickly after windy days.
  • Filter gauge. Write down the pressure reading so the company can compare it with a clean filter.
  • Equipment pad. Check for drips or damp ground around the pump, filter and pipes.
  • Breaker. Reset a tripped breaker or GFCI once. If it trips again, leave it off and call.

Safety first. Never mix pool chemicals with each other. Keep them apart, dry, shaded and out of children's reach, and follow the label. Keep children away from a pool without a fence or cover, and treat a green or murky pool as a drowning hazard since no one can see the bottom. If you smell gas near a pool heater, leave the area, do not touch switches or the heater, and from a safe distance call 911 or the emergency number on your gas bill.

Reading your provider's report, and questions to ask

The City of Buckeye posts its Annual Water Quality Report on its Environmental Compliance page. Each year's report covers the tests from the year before. If another provider serves your home, read that provider's report instead. Look for the mineral rows, such as hardness and TDS, then compare them with your own pool test over a few months. For power, check the utility named on your bill; it handles outages, not pool equipment. Before draining, read how often to drain a pool in Arizona.

Questions to ask a Buckeye pool company

  • What is your Arizona ROC license number? Needed for repair, equipment and resurfacing work. Check it on the ROC search yourself.
  • Do you test TDS as well as calcium? With well water, both numbers tell you where the pool is heading.
  • How do you keep scale off the tile and out of the heater or salt cell? Ask what the plan is, not just the price.
  • If draining is needed, how much and where does it go? A partial drain may be enough and uses less water.
  • What is on the written quote? Labor, parts and chemicals, listed before work starts.
