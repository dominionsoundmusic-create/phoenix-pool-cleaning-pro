Pool resurfacing in Phoenix means stripping or roughening the worn inside layer of a concrete pool and applying a fresh finish over the shell. It is not a cleaning job and not a quick patch. The pool is drained, the old surface is prepared, a new layer of plaster, quartz, pebble or tile goes on, and the pool is refilled and nursed through its first weeks. Done well, it gives the pool a new skin that should last for many seasons. Done in a hurry, it can flake, stain or crack early.

Signs your pool surface is due

A pool finish wears out slowly, so many owners only notice when someone scrapes a foot or snags a swimsuit. Walk the pool with the water clear and the sun high, and look for these:

  • Rough, sandpaper texture. The smooth top of the finish has worn away, leaving grit that catches skin and fabric.
  • Etching. A pitted, chalky or blotchy look, often worst on steps and benches, where the surface has been eaten into.
  • Stains that come back. Brown, gray or mottled patches that return soon after a careful cleaning, or that never lift at all.
  • Delamination. Areas that sound hollow when tapped, blister, or peel away in flakes, because the finish has let go of the layer under it.
  • Thin or exposed spots. Dark patches where the base coat or the concrete shell is showing through.
  • A web of fine cracks across large areas, rather than one small line in one spot.

How a resurfacing job runs, step by step

Every company has its own routine, but a careful job follows roughly this order. Knowing it helps you read a quote and ask the right questions.

  1. Inspection and written quote. Someone looks at the whole interior, the waterline tile, the coping stones around the edge and the fittings, then puts the scope and price in writing.
  2. Draining. The pool is pumped out. Where that water goes is set by your city, not by the company's habit, so ask how they will handle it under your city's rule before the day arrives.
  3. Prep. Loose and hollow finish is removed, and the remaining surface is chipped, ground or roughened so the new layer can bond. Fittings, lights and drains are protected or reset. Prep is where cheap jobs often cut corners, and poor prep is a common reason a new finish lets go early.
  4. Repairs found once it is empty. Cracks, a damaged tile line or loose coping often show up only after the water is gone. Any added work should come to you as a written change order that both sides sign before it is done.
  5. Applying the new finish. The crew applies and trowels the new material while it is workable. Timing matters here, which is why the work tends to happen in one long push.
  6. Refilling. Water usually goes back in soon after the finish is applied. Ask the company how fast it wants the pool filled and whether the hose should run without stopping.
  7. Startup and the first weeks. A new cement-based finish releases fine dust as it cures. Expect instructions for frequent brushing and careful water balancing during the early weeks, and get that startup routine in writing so you know who does what.

Why draining in the summer heat matters

A drained pool in a Valley summer is a concrete basin sitting in full sun. That shapes the job in a few practical ways, and a good company will raise them before you do.

  • Timing within the day. Ask what time work starts and how the crew keeps the new finish from drying too fast on a hot afternoon.
  • How long the shell sits empty. Every extra day empty is a day of sun on bare surfaces and a day the pool is unusable. Ask for the expected number of days from drain to refill.
  • Dust and storms. Summer storms can blow dirt, leaves and grit into an empty or freshly filled pool. Ask how the company protects fresh work if a storm rolls in.
  • Refill water. Filling a pool takes a lot of tap water, and Valley water is hard. Ask how the company will balance the fresh fill so it does not start scaling or etching the new surface.
  • Safety. An empty pool is a long fall onto concrete. Keep children and pets out of the yard while it is drained, and keep any gate latched.

Some owners prefer to schedule the job in the cooler months for these reasons. Whatever the season, the questions above are worth asking.

What a pool resurfacing quote should list

A one-line price on a business card is not a quote. Before you agree to resurfacing, the written estimate should spell out:

  • The finish type, color and thickness, in plain words.
  • How the old surface will be prepared, and whether any of it is being fully removed.
  • Draining and refilling: who does it, and where the drained water will go.
  • What happens with the waterline tile, coping, lights, steps and fittings: kept, cleaned, reset or replaced.
  • How hidden repairs will be priced and approved, ideally with a signed change order.
  • The startup routine after refill and who carries it out.
  • The payment schedule. The Registrar of Contractors advises not letting payments get ahead of the work.
  • The company's name exactly as it appears on its ROC license, and the license number.
  • Any written workmanship or materials warranty, with what it covers and for how long.

The ROC also suggests getting written estimates from at least three contractors and checking that the contract matches the estimate before you sign. Our guide to choosing a pool company in Phoenix covers contracts, reviews and red flags in more depth.

Licensing: who may replace a pool finish in Arizona

Arizona's Registrar of Contractors (ROC) sorts contractors into license classifications. Its residential R-6 Swimming Pool Service and Repair license allows servicing and minor repair of residential pools. The ROC states plainly that R-6 does not allow a complete replacement of plaster or pebble pool interiors and decks. A full resurface needs a contractor whose license covers that scope, such as the residential B-5 swimming pool contractor classification, which the ROC describes as building and repairing pools and spas.

So the useful question is not just "are you licensed?" but "which classification do you hold, and does it cover a full resurface?" Look the number up on the ROC search and read the classification listed. If the answer is unclear, the ROC can tell you which license fits the job.

Pool deck repair and resurfacing in Phoenix

The deck around a pool takes the same sun as the water and usually more foot traffic. Cracked, flaking or hot, faded coatings are common reasons owners ask about deck work while the pool is being redone. Some pool companies also coat or repair decks; others focus only on the interior and refer the deck out. Either way, the deck should be its own line on the quote. Keep in mind that the R-6 service and repair license does not allow a complete replacement of decks, so ask what license covers the deck portion as well.

These are published estimates, not prices from this line, and real Phoenix quotes vary widely.

What moves the number:

  • Pool size and shape. More square feet of floor, wall, steps and benches means more material and labor.
  • The finish. Pebble and tile generally cost more than plaster.
  • Prep. A full removal of the old finish costs more than roughening a sound surface.
  • Extras. New waterline tile, coping, lights or crack repairs found once the pool is empty.
  • Deck work, if it is added to the same project.

Keeping a new finish looking new

Most of what shortens a finish happens in the water, not on the trowel. Water that is out of balance can slowly dissolve a cement-based surface or coat it in scale. Regular testing and brushing, and a filter that is kept clean, do more for a new surface than any product. The pool cleaning and weekly maintenance page explains the routine, and pool tile cleaning covers the white calcium line that forms at the waterline in the Valley.
