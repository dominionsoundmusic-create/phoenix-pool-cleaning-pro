A weekly pool cleaning visit, step by step

Order matters. Debris comes out before brushing, brushing comes before vacuuming, and testing comes last so the reading reflects water that has been cleaned and circulated. A careful technician tends to work in a sequence like this:

  1. Look first. Before touching anything, a good technician notes water color and clarity, the water level against the skimmer, any stains or algae spots, and whether the pump is running and sounds normal.
  2. Skim the surface. A leaf net on a long pole lifts floating leaves, bugs, palo verde blossoms and anything else before it sinks and stains the floor.
  3. Empty the baskets. The skimmer basket and the pump's strainer basket are emptied with the pump off. Full baskets choke water flow, which makes the filter and chlorine work less well.
  4. Brush walls, steps and the tile line. Brushing loosens dust and early algae before it can grip the plaster. Corners, steps and the area behind ladders get extra attention because circulation is weakest there.
  5. Vacuum the floor. Fine dust that settled after brushing is vacuumed up. If the pool has an automatic cleaner, the technician checks that it is moving freely and empties its bag or canister.
  6. Check the filter pressure. The gauge on the filter tank shows how hard the pump is pushing. A reading well above the clean baseline means the filter is loaded and due for cleaning. See the pool filter cleaning page for more.
  7. Test and balance the water. Free chlorine and pH every visit, and on a regular schedule total alkalinity, calcium hardness and cyanuric acid. Chemicals are added based on the readings, not by habit.
  8. Note and report. A short log of readings, chemicals added and anything unusual at the equipment pad helps you see trends and catch problems early.

The water targets a careful technician aims for

Clear water is not always safe water, and safe water can still damage plaster and equipment if it is out of balance. These are the main readings on a weekly test and why each one matters in Phoenix.

Total alkalinity, which steadies pH, is also tested on a schedule. Your technician may also check the water's overall balance with a saturation index, a calculation that combines pH, alkalinity, calcium, temperature and dissolved solids to show whether water is likely to leave scale or eat into surfaces.

Why pool cleaning in Phoenix, Arizona needs a weekly rhythm

Hard water and rising minerals. The City of Phoenix draws water from the Salt and Verde rivers, the Colorado River through the Central Arizona Project and groundwater, and its 2025 water quality report lists total hardness of 172 to 302 parts per million, about 10 to 17.6 grains per gallon. Water evaporates but minerals do not, so each top-off adds more. Weekly brushing and testing are how a technician keeps that from turning into crust at the tile line.

Evaporation. The Arizona Department of Water Resources estimates that a standard uncovered pool loses four to six feet of water a year to evaporation, mostly in summer, and that a 7 mile per hour breeze across the surface can raise evaporation by 300 percent. That is a lot of refill water, and a lot of minerals left behind.

Heat and UV. Phoenix spent 143 days at or above 100 degrees in 2024, the National Weather Service reports. Warm water and strong sunlight use up chlorine quickly, which is why most outdoor pools here rely on stabilizer and why a week between tests is about as long as most pools can go in summer.

Dust and storms. From June 15 to September 30, the National Weather Service's monsoon period, thunderstorm outflows can drop a layer of fine dust and debris into the water in minutes. That dust clouds the water, feeds algae and loads the filter. The monsoon pool care guide covers storm cleanup in detail.

Salt pools and chlorine pools

Traditional chlorine pools

Chlorine is added as tablets in a feeder or floater, as liquid, or both. Stabilized tablets add cyanuric acid every time they dissolve, so a technician keeps an eye on that reading and changes the approach if it climbs too high. The weekly visit is mostly about keeping chlorine steady through the heat.

Salt water pools

A salt system passes a low electric current through slightly salty water to make chlorine in the cell. The pool still needs skimming, brushing, vacuuming and pH and alkalinity checks. In hard water, white scale can gather on the cell plates and lower its output, so the cell should be looked at on a regular schedule. Cell cleaning involves acid and is a job for the technician.

Either way, the physical cleaning is the same. A salt system replaces the chlorine jug, not the weekly visit.

What you can safely do between visits

A few minutes a week keeps a pool in better shape for the next service visit. These steps involve no chemical mixing and no electrical work:

  • Skim floating leaves and bugs with a leaf net.
  • Empty the skimmer basket after windy days, with the pump switched off.
  • Keep the water level around the middle of the skimmer opening, especially in summer.
  • Note the filter pressure right after a cleaning and watch for a large rise.
  • Hose dust off the deck away from the pool after a storm so less of it washes in.
  • Test the water with a home kit if you want to follow along, and share the numbers with your technician.

Chemical safety. Never mix pool chemicals with each other, and never pour one product into another product's container. The CDC advises keeping chlorine and acid apart, storing chemicals closed in their original labeled containers somewhere cool, ventilated and out of direct sun, locked away from children and pets, and following the label exactly. Leave acid work to a professional.

When cleaning turns into something else

Weekly cleaning is maintenance. Some things a technician spots on a visit are not:

  • Water that keeps going green or cloudy even with regular care can point to a circulation, filter or chemistry problem. The green pool cleanup page explains how a neglected pool is brought back.
  • A pump that hums, leaks or will not prime belongs on the pool pump repair page.
  • Heavy white crust at the waterline that brushing will not move may need professional pool tile cleaning.
  • Water loss well beyond normal evaporation could be a leak.

A neglected, green pool is also a public health and safety concern. The bottom cannot be seen, which makes it a drowning hazard, and standing water can breed mosquitoes. Maricopa County Environmental Services takes mosquito complaints at 602-506-6616, and its fiscal year 2025 annual report counts 228 green pools treated as part of its mosquito control work.
