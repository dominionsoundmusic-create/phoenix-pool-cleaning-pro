Scottsdale: one long city with three kinds of tap water

Scottsdale runs a long way north from its southern edge near Tempe, past Old Town, McCormick Ranch and Gainey Ranch, up through Kierland and Grayhawk to DC Ranch, McDowell Mountain Ranch and the Pinnacle Peak area at the foot of the McDowell Mountains. Scottsdale Water, the city's own utility, serves that whole stretch, and its water is not the same from one end to the other.

The city says roughly 90 percent of its drinking water now comes from surface water, mostly Colorado River water delivered by the Central Arizona Project (CAP), plus Salt and Verde river water from the Salt River Project (SRP). That is a big change: before the mid 1980s, Scottsdale depended entirely on groundwater, and it first took CAP water in 1987. Wells now supply less than 10 percent. Because hardness depends on which source and which treatment plant feeds an area, a pool in south Scottsdale and a pool in the north can scale at very different rates.

Hardness by neighborhood, from the city's own fact sheet

Hardness is the amount of dissolved calcium and magnesium in the water. Scottsdale Water reports it in grains per gallon (gpg) and in milligrams per liter (mg/L, about the same as parts per million). Its hard water fact sheet splits the city into three bands using two familiar roads as the lines:

Approximate tap water hardness in Scottsdale by area, from Scottsdale Water
Part of ScottsdaleGrains per gallonMilligrams per liter
South of Indian School Road22 to 25370 to 430
Indian School Road to Chaparral Road20 to 22340 to 370
North of Chaparral Road16 to 18275 to 300

The city points out that hardness is not a health standard and is not considered a health risk. A pool is a different story. Water leaves by evaporation all summer, the minerals stay behind, and every top-up from the hose brings in more. In the southern band that cycle starts from a higher number, so the white crust at the waterline, cloudy water after a heat wave and scale inside a heater or salt cell can show up sooner. In the north the tap water is softer, but it is still hard, and the same buildup happens over more seasons.

Knowing your band helps when you talk to a pool company. Ask how it tracks calcium hardness and total dissolved solids (TDS, everything dissolved in the water, including salt and stabilizer) at your address, and at what point it would suggest a partial drain and refill. The pool tile cleaning page explains what scale on the tile line is and how it comes off, and the guide to draining a pool in Arizona covers when fresh water makes sense.

Draining a Scottsdale pool: where the water may go

Scottsdale says plainly that pool water must not go to the street, an alley, other city right of way, a storm drain inlet or a drainage channel.

Onto your own landscape

You may use pool water on your yard as long as it soaks in and stays on your property. The city suggests draining over a few days and testing first: pH between 6.5 and 8.5 and chlorine below 0.1 mg/L, to lessen harm to plants.

Into the sewer cleanout

If there is more water than the yard can take, the city allows draining to the sanitary sewer through the home's cleanout, at no more than 50 gallons a minute. Secure the hose, and check the tub and shower right away. If water backs up, shut the pump off.

Never to the curb

No draining down the driveway, into the gutter or onto a neighbor's lot. A pool company that offers to drain your pool should say exactly where the water will go, and the answer should be one of the two options the city allows.

Older homes, missing cleanouts and the winter sewer bill

Scottsdale describes a sewer cleanout as two four inch caps about a foot apart with a square nut on top, and it notes that the city keeps no record of where cleanouts sit on private property. If you cannot find yours, sort that out with a plumber before the day you plan to drain, which is worth doing early in the older neighborhoods of south and central Scottsdale.

Timing matters too. Scottsdale bases the residential sewer volume charge on water use billed in December, January and February, so refilling a pool in those months can push your sewer bill up for the whole year. The city lets you request an adjustment, with a deadline of August 31. Ask the pool company to plan a drain and refill around that, and keep a note of the refill date and how much water it took.

Safe checks before you call

  • Baskets. Empty the skimmer and pump baskets. After a dust storm they can fill in a single afternoon.
  • Water level. Look at the skimmer opening. In summer, evaporation can drop the level until the pump pulls air.
  • Filter gauge. Note the pressure reading and tell the company. A reading well above normal usually means a dirty filter.
  • Breaker or GFCI. Reset a tripped one once. If it trips again, keep it off and mention that when you phone.
  • Children. Keep kids away from a pool with cloudy or green water. You cannot see the bottom, which makes it a drowning hazard.

Gas smell at a pool heater? Leave the area, keep others away and do not touch the heater, switches or controls. From a safe distance, call 911 and Southwest Gas at 877-860-6020. Never mix pool chemicals with each other, and store them apart, dry, shaded and out of children's reach.

Short-term rental pools and Scottsdale's barrier rules

Scottsdale licenses short-term and vacation rentals. Under the city's short-term rental ordinance, owners need an annual city license for each Scottsdale property, on top of a state transaction privilege tax license and registration with the Maricopa County Assessor. Each rental must name a real person as its emergency contact, with a 24 hour phone number.

Rental pools get their own safety rules, and the city says they apply no matter how old the house or the pool is. A rental with a pool, spa or hot tub that guests can use needs:

  • A perimeter fenceat least 60 inches high, hard to climb, that keeps the public out of the backyard, such as a block wall.
  • Gates that close and latch by themselveswith the latch at least 54 inches up and able to take a lock. Vehicle gates must be locked.
  • A second barriereither a separate 60 inch pool fence or audible alarms on the doors and windows that lead to the pool.
  • A lockable spa coverwhen a spa or hot tub sits outside the pool fence and the doors and windows to it have no alarms.

For homes that are not rentals, Scottsdale's 2021 pool code amendments also call for a barrier at least 60 inches high, and they make the owner responsible for keeping gates, fencing, doors, locks, alarms and latches in good working order. Which version applies to an older pool can depend on when it was permitted, so check with the city's Inspection Services at 480-312-2500 before you change a fence.
