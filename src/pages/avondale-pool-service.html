Avondale: two water sources in one pool

The City of Avondale runs its own water utility. Part of the supply is groundwater, pumped from a network of city wells that reach the West Salt River Valley Sub-Basin aquifer. The other part is surface water from the Colorado River, which Avondale receives under an agreement with the City of Phoenix. Phoenix delivers Avondale's allocation through its regional pipeline system, and the city blends it with well water before it reaches homes.

For a pool owner, the practical point is that you are not working with one fixed kind of water. Groundwater and river water carry different amounts of dissolved minerals, so the water you add in July may not match what you added in March. A pool that held steady for years can start leaving white crust at the waterline after a long run of hot days and heavy top offs. That is a good reason to have the pool water itself tested on a regular schedule rather than relying on a number from a report.

Reading Avondale's water report for your pool

The city publishes its water test results once a year in a Consumer Confidence Report. The report covering January through December 2025 was posted in June 2026.

Where to find it

The Water Quality page on the city's website links each year's report, with older reports kept in an archive. Paper copies are available at Avondale City Hall and local libraries.

What to look for

Look for the rows that describe minerals, such as hardness, sodium or total dissolved solids (TDS), the overall amount of minerals and salts dissolved in the water. For a pool, these are the figures that shape how fast scale forms.

What it cannot tell you

The report describes water leaving the system, not water that has sat in your pool for two summers. Evaporation leaves minerals behind, so the pool's own test numbers matter more than the tap figures.

When the water bill and the pool both look wrong

If your pool has an automatic filler, it is a valve with a float that adds water whenever the level drops. It saves you from dragging a hose out every few days. It can also hide a problem. The city's utility billing FAQ points out that pool fillers, water softeners, water filters and water heaters all have floats that can stick open and run water you never see. Its suggestion is simple: turn the device off and repeat the meter test. If the usage stops, you have found the source.

That matters more now that water costs more. Avondale adopted new water, sewer and solid waste rates that took effect January 1, 2026, and the city adds a 7.5 percent water treatment fee and a 6.2 percent water resource fee to the total water bill. A filler stuck open, or a slow leak in the plumbing, is paying those fees on water that ends up in the ground.

Safe checks before you call

  • Water level. Note where the water sits on the skimmer opening, then check again in a day with the filler turned off.
  • Equipment pad. Look for drips, damp soil or white mineral trails on the pump, filter and pipes.
  • Baskets and gauge. Empty the skimmer and pump baskets and write down the filter pressure reading.
  • Breaker. If the pump is dead, reset a tripped breaker or GFCI once. If it trips again, leave it off and call.
  • Home test kit. Test the water and keep the numbers to read out on the call.

Safety first. Never mix pool chemicals with each other, and store them apart, dry, shaded and out of children's reach. Keep children away from any pool that is not fenced or covered. A cloudy or green pool is a drowning hazard because no one can see the bottom. If you smell gas near a pool heater, leave the area, do not touch switches or the heater, and from a safe distance call 911 or the emergency number on your gas bill.
