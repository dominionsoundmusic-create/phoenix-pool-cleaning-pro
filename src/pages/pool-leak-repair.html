Pool leak repair in Phoenix starts with a question most owners in milder places never have to ask: is this pool actually leaking, or is the desert just drinking it? Valley pools lose a surprising amount of water to the air every summer. A leak hides inside that loss until the numbers stop adding up.

This page shows how to tell the two apart, how to run the bucket test safely, where leaks usually hide, what a leak detection visit involves, and how patches and crack repairs work. It also covers who in Arizona is licensed for which repairs.

Evaporation or a leak in a Phoenix summer

Start with what normal looks like. The Arizona Department of Water Resources puts average evaporation in Phoenix at about six feet of water a year, most of it in summer. A City of Mesa chart for a 400 square foot pool adds up to about 79 inches a year, close to 20,000 gallons. A similar table from the City of Scottsdale shows the loss peaking at about 10.8 inches in June, which works out to roughly a third of an inch a day, and falling to about 2.6 inches in January.

So a pool that drops a quarter or a third of an inch on a hot, dry, breezy June day may be perfectly sound. Water features, a spa spillover, heavy splashing and wind all add to the loss. What should get your attention is a drop that is clearly bigger than the season explains, a drop that continues in winter when evaporation is low, or a pool that loses more when the pump is running than when it is off.

Leaks are not rare. The City of Mesa estimates that up to 30 percent of pools may have a leak. An autofill can hide one for months, with the only clue a higher water bill, which is one more reason to check the meter or the bill against last year's.

The bucket test, step by step

This is the standard way to separate evaporation from a leak, and it needs nothing more than a bucket and a marker. The bucket and the pool sit in the same sun and wind, so they should lose about the same amount to evaporation.

  1. Turn off the autofill if the pool has one, so it cannot top the pool up during the test.
  2. Fill a bucket with pool water and set it on the first or second step, so the bucket's water level is close to the pool's.
  3. Mark both levels. Mark the water line inside the bucket, and mark the pool's level on the tile or skimmer with tape or a grease pencil.
  4. Run the pump on its normal schedule and leave everything alone for two to three days. Calm, dry days give the cleanest result; rain or strong wind can throw it off.
  5. Compare the drops. If the pool fell noticeably more than the bucket, the pool is losing water some other way.
  6. Optional second round: the City of Mesa suggests measuring the loss over 24 hours with the pump running, then over 24 hours with it off. If more water goes with the pump running, the plumbing is the likely cause; if the loss is about the same either way, the shell, liner or fittings become more likely.

Keep children away from the pool while you work around it, and never leave a child alone near the water. Do not climb in to inspect anything while the pump is running.

Where pool leaks hide

What pool leak detection involves

Leak detection is a process of narrowing things down. A careful pool technician usually works from the simplest checks to the more involved ones.

  1. Confirm the loss. Review the bucket test, the water bill or the autofill, and how the level behaves with the pump on and off.
  2. Look over the equipment and deck. Many leaks turn out to be at the pad.
  3. Dye testing. With the water still, a small amount of test dye is released right next to fittings, cracks, lights and the skimmer. If the dye is pulled into a gap, water is leaving there. The dye has to be very close to the leak to show it, so this often means the technician gets in the pool.
  4. Pressure testing. Each plumbing line is plugged and pressurized. A line that will not hold pressure is leaking somewhere along its length.
  5. Listening equipment. Electronic listening devices on the deck, or hydrophones in the water, help pinpoint where a line is leaking underground, so less deck has to be opened. The City of Mesa suggests asking whether a company uses tools such as sonar, infrared thermography or ultrasound.

Ask for a written summary of what was tested and what was found. If a line has to be dug up, ask how much deck will be cut and how it will be patched.

Patching a pool: what works with the water in

Small leaks in the shell or around fittings are often fixed without draining. Two part epoxy putties sold for pools are made to harden underwater; the area is cleaned, the putty is mixed and pressed into the crack or around the fitting, then smoothed. It is quick and keeps the pool full, which matters in a city that asks residents to drain pools only rarely. Larger cracks, skimmer replacement and buried plumbing are bigger jobs.

Good candidates for an underwater patch

  • A small crack or chip that dye shows is leaking
  • A gap between a fitting and the plaster
  • The joint where the skimmer meets the shell
  • A small hole or tear in a vinyl liner, with a vinyl patch

When a patch is not enough

  • Plaster that is soft, hollow or flaking over a wide area
  • A crack that keeps growing or runs through the shell
  • A leak in buried plumbing
  • Several leaks across an old, worn surface

When the surface itself is worn out, the answer may be replastering or another new interior rather than one more patch. Our pool resurfacing page explains those options, and the guide on whether to repair or resurface a pool helps you weigh the cost of patches against a new surface.

Can a cracked pool be repaired?

Most cracked pools can be repaired. The question is what kind of crack it is. The National Plasterers Council describes closed hairline shrinkage cracks and crazing (a network of fine surface cracks) as cosmetic. Open cracks, and cracks that run all the way through the finish because the structure underneath is moving, need remedial work, and patching them without stopping the movement is a poor fix. A crack that leaks, widens, shows a step from one side to the other, or runs along the tile line and bond beam calls for a diagnosis of the cause before a lasting repair is chosen.

Licensing for leak, plumbing and crack repair in Arizona

Arizona's Registrar of Contractors (ROC) licenses contractors, and the classification matters for leak work. The ROC's R-6 Swimming Pool Service and Repair classification covers servicing pools and minor repairs, but it lists plumbing connections to a drinking water system, gas lines and electrical work beyond the first disconnect as outside its scope, and it does not allow a complete replacement of a plaster or pebble interior or the deck. So a pool company may patch a fitting under its R-6 license, while larger shell, plumbing or replastering work may call for a different classification. Ask the company which license covers the work it is quoting, then confirm it on the ROC contractor search.

Questions worth asking before the leak work starts

  • How will you find the leak, and is detection priced separately from the repair?
  • What did the dye and pressure tests show, and can I see it?
  • Is this crack cosmetic or structural, and how do you know?
  • Can it be patched with the water in, or does the level need to come down?
  • If water must be removed, where will it go?
  • What is your ROC license number and classification?
