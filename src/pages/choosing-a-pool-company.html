Searching for pool companies in Phoenix turns up hundreds of names, and most of them say similar things. What separates a good fit from a costly mistake is rarely the advertising. It is whether the company does the kind of work you need, holds the right Arizona license when the work calls for one, writes down what it will do, and handles money the way the state's contractor agency recommends. This guide shows you how to check each of those yourself, without relying on anyone's word for it, including ours.

Three kinds of pool companies in the Valley

"Pool company" covers very different businesses. Knowing which kind you need narrows the search before you look at a single review.

Weekly service routes

Skimming, brushing, emptying baskets, testing and adjusting the water on a set day each week. Often a small operator with a route of regular homes. Look for weekly cleaning and maintenance or a full pool service plan.

Repair and equipment

Pumps, filters, heaters, salt systems, valves, automation and leaks. This is contracting work in Arizona, so the license question matters here. See pool repair and pool equipment repair.

Resurfacing and remodeling

New interior finishes, tile, coping and decks, sometimes larger rebuilds. These jobs cost the most and need a contractor whose license covers them. See pool resurfacing.

Some companies do all three; many do one well and refer out the rest. Ask early which parts of your job the company does with its own people and which it hands to someone else.

Look up a pool company on the ROC contractor search, step by step

The ROC's contractor search is free and public. It takes a couple of minutes and tells you things no advertisement or review will.

  1. Get the details from the company. Ask for the license number and the exact business name on the license. A company doing repair or resurfacing work should give both without hesitation.
  2. Open the search. Go to the ROC contractor search (opens in a new tab) and search by license number, or by business name if you do not have the number yet.
  3. Check the status. The license should be active. A license that is suspended, revoked, expired or inactive is a reason to stop.
  4. Read the classification. Match it to your job using the table above: R-6, C-6 or CR-6 for service and repair, a classification such as B-5 for pool building and resurfacing work.
  5. Compare the name. The name on the license should match the name on the quote, the contract and the checks you write.
  6. Look at the bond and complaint history. The record shows bond information and complaints. One old complaint that was resolved is different from a pattern of recent ones; read the details.
  7. Check again when you sign. The ROC advises making sure the license is in good standing on the day you sign the contract, not just when you first got the quote.

The Residential Contractors' Recovery Fund

Arizona has a fund, run by the ROC, that can help some homeowners who are harmed by a licensed residential contractor's poor or unfinished work. It is a strong reason to hire licensed for repairs and resurfacing, because it only covers work by a contractor who was properly licensed at the time.

  • Who can claim: generally a person who owns the home and lives in it or intends to, plus some trusts, associations and tenants in narrower cases.
  • Which contractors: the contractor must have held a valid residential license on the date the contract was signed, the first payment was made or the work began.
  • How it starts: through an ROC complaint that leads to action against the license, or through a court case and order. The ROC says you must also pursue the contractor's bond first.
  • Limits: awards are capped by statute and cannot exceed your actual damages. The fund does not pay everyone who applies, and deadlines apply, so read the ROC's page early if you think you may need it.

How to read online reviews sensibly

Searches like "Arizona pool service reviews" or "best pool repair Phoenix" lead to star ratings, lists and forum threads. They are worth reading, but they work best as one piece of evidence among several.

  • Read the middle, not just the extremes. Three- and four-star reviews often describe what a company is actually like on an ordinary week.
  • Match the job. Praise for weekly cleaning says little about how a company handles a heater repair or a full resurface.
  • Look at the dates. Pool companies change owners and staff. Recent reviews describe the business you would hire.
  • Watch how complaints are answered. A calm, specific reply that offers to fix things is a good sign. Silence or arguing is not.
  • Be wary of "best of" lists. Some are paid placements. Treat them as a starting list of names, then check each one yourself.
  • Check what reviews cannot show. License status, classification and complaints filed with the state are on the ROC search, not on a review site.

Put it in writing

Whether you are signing up for weekly service or approving a repair, a written agreement protects both sides. The ROC recommends a written contract that spells out the price, who is responsible for any permits and the payment schedule, and suggests getting written estimates from at least three contractors for contracting work.

A weekly service agreement should cover

  • The visit day and what each visit includes
  • Whether chemicals are billed separately or within the price
  • How filter cleaning and other periodic tasks are handled and charged
  • How you will hear about problems found at a visit, and how repairs are approved
  • How either side can end the agreement

A repair or resurfacing contract should cover

  • The business name and ROC license number
  • The exact work, parts or finish, and the total price
  • Who pulls any permits
  • A payment schedule that does not run ahead of the work
  • How changes are handled: the ROC says change orders must be signed by both parties
  • Any written warranty, what it covers and for how long

Red flags when choosing a pool company

Most of these come straight from the ROC's advice to homeowners; the rest are plain common sense.

Slow down if you see

  • A request for cash, or a check made out to a person rather than the company on the contract
  • Full or most of the payment wanted before work starts, when no special-order materials justify it
  • No written quote or contract, or a contract that does not match the quote
  • No license number offered for repair or resurfacing work, or a name that does not match the ROC record
  • Pressure to decide on the spot, or a "today only" price
  • Extra work started without a signed change order

Good signs

  • A license number given freely, with a classification that fits the job
  • A clear written quote that explains what was found and why the work is needed
  • A payment schedule tied to progress
  • Straight answers about who does each part of the job
  • Time to think it over and compare
