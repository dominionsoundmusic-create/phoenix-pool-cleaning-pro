Mesa: one city, several water zones

The City of Mesa serves drinking water from both surface water and groundwater wells, and its annual report explains that the wells themselves differ in how many minerals they carry. Instead of one citywide number, the 2025 report, issued in 2026, sorts hardness by zone. Homes in the City Zone average about 12 grains per gallon. Homes in the Eastern and Southern zones average about 16. Across the whole city, Mesa's water quality page puts the range at 12 to 22 grains per gallon, depending on which source reaches your tap.

To compare that with a pool test strip, multiply grains by 17.1 to get parts per million. Twelve grains is roughly 205 parts per million of hardness; sixteen grains is roughly 274. Those figures describe the fill water before a single gallon evaporates. If you live in southeast Mesa, in the 85212 ZIP code or nearby, and are not sure which zone serves you, the report includes a map and a hardness table, and Mesa Water Quality Services answers questions at 480-644-6461.

Draining a pool the way Mesa asks

Mesa's environmental team publishes step by step guidance for draining or backwashing a pool or spa. The short version: the sewer cleanout on your property is the place for it, and storm drains are not.

Use the sewer cleanout

No notification or permit is needed to drain to the sanitary sewer cleanout on your own lot, usually a black threaded cap near the house. If there are two, the city says to use the one closest to the home. Mesa then treats that water and reuses it for landscape irrigation or groundwater recharge.

Go slow and watch inside

The city recommends about 12 gallons per minute and water near neutral, a pH of 6 to 8. A pump that is too strong can push water back into the yard or house, so check the shower and tub drains after it starts and shut it off if water backs up. Keep sand, soil and debris out of the line.

Keep it out of the street

Mesa discourages sending pool water to streets, curbs, gutters or storm drain inlets, and says doing so may violate city code. A permanent drain line from the pool to the sewer is also off limits, since it may break plumbing or county health rules.

Two Mesa details that catch owners out

The winter refill and your sewer fee

Mesa calculates the wastewater part of a home's bill from water use in winter. Because of that, the city notes that filling a pool between December and March can raise your sewer fee, while filling between April and November does not. If a pool company recommends draining and refilling to bring down calcium, total dissolved solids (the minerals and salts that build up as water evaporates) or stabilizer, ask whether the job can be scheduled outside those winter months.

Acid washing and replastering wastewater

The city does not allow wastewater from pool repairs such as acid washing or replastering to go to the storm sewer or the sanitary sewer, and it puts the responsibility on the property owner to make sure the repair company disposes of it properly. Before a resurfacing or acid wash, ask the company exactly what happens to that water and get the answer in writing on the quote. If you are deciding between patching and a full new surface, the repair or resurface guide walks through it.

Pool water on the landscape

Mesa cautions that pool water holds more salt and chlorine than tap water, so take care before letting it run onto planting beds or trees. A saltwater pool carries even more dissolved salt, so ask the company before any of it goes on the yard.

Hard water, heat and a Mesa pool

Even at the city's lower zone average, a pool in the Valley concentrates minerals every time you top it off after a hot week, because the water evaporates and the calcium stays. A careful pool technician tests calcium hardness and total dissolved solids along with the daily basics, and uses the Langelier Saturation Index (LSI), a calculation that combines pH, alkalinity, calcium and water temperature, to judge whether the water is likely to leave scale or etch plaster. Hot summer water pushes that balance toward scale.

Where your zone runs harder, the margin is smaller. Expect the company you are put through to to talk about how often to test, what the tile line looks like, and when partial or full water replacement makes sense. The guide to draining a pool in Arizona explains the signs.

  • Baskets first. Clear the skimmer and pump baskets; a clogged basket can starve the pump.
  • Read the filter gauge. Note the pressure and tell the company; high pressure usually points to a dirty filter.
  • Check the schedule. Make sure the pump timer or automation did not reset after an outage.
  • Look at the pad. Note drips or puddles around the pump, filter and heater, but do not open anything electrical.
  • Breaker once. Reset a tripped pool breaker or GFCI one time only. A second trip means leave it off and call.
  • Chemical safety. Never mix pool chemicals with each other, keep them apart, dry and shaded and out of reach of children, and follow the label.

Gas smell near a pool heater? Move away from it, leave switches and the heater alone, and once you are clear of the area phone 911 or your gas utility. Keep children away from any pool that is unfenced or uncovered, and never leave a child alone near water.
