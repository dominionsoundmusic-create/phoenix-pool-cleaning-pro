Litchfield Park: a company town that grew around its trees

Litchfield Park is older than most of its neighbors. The city's general plan says it was established in 1916 and incorporated as a city in 1987. It began as a farm town for the Goodyear Tire and Rubber Company, which needed long-staple cotton for tire cords during World War I, and executive Paul Litchfield led the land purchases. The town took his name officially in 1926. The Wigwam opened to the public as a resort in 1929, after starting as guest quarters for visiting company executives.

The trees are the part a pool owner notices. The city's general plan calls its alternating palm and orange tree pattern the trademark of the community, and Litchfield Park was named a 2025 Tree City USA. A newer pocket of homes sits in The Village at Litchfield Park and in developments west of Litchfield Road, but much of the town is older housing set among mature landscaping. The 2020 Census counted 6,847 residents, a small city surrounded by much larger ones.

Leaves, fronds and blossoms: what old trees do to a pool

A yard with tall palms and citrus is pleasant to sit in and hard on a pool. Leaves sink and stain plaster if they sit. Palm fronds and seed clusters jam skimmer baskets. Citrus blossoms in spring and fruit that drops over the wall add organic matter that eats up chlorine, so the water can turn cloudy faster than in a bare desert yard. Debris that reaches the pump basket slows the flow, and a filter loaded with fine plant matter has to be cleaned more often.

None of that is a reason to cut down the trees the city is proud of. It is a reason to set up service that fits the yard. A careful pool technician working under heavy trees usually empties baskets every visit, brushes the walls and floor where leaves settle, checks the filter pressure gauge closely and tells you if the skimmer or pump basket is cracked. Ask how the company handles a yard like yours before you agree to a schedule.

  • Visit frequency. A yard that drops leaves every day may need weekly visits even in winter.
  • Baskets and lid seals. A torn basket lets debris into the pump. A worn lid seal lets the pump pull air.
  • Filter cleaning. Ask how often the filter is cleaned and whether that is part of the price. The pool filter cleaning page explains why it matters.
  • Leaf covers or nets. Ask whether one makes sense for fall and spring, and remember any cover must be fully off before anyone swims.

What Liberty's water report says about hardness

The city does not run the water system. Litchfield Park's utility page says water and sewer service come from Liberty Utilities, formerly the Litchfield Park Service Company, which it notes is not a department of the city. Liberty's reports say it pumps groundwater from the Western Valley Salt River Aquifer, mostly from layers about 200 to 600 feet down.

Hardness: 150 to 360 ppm

Liberty's 2026 report, covering 2025 testing on its Litchfield Park and Goodyear system, gives an average of 233 ppm as calcium carbonate. In grains per gallon, that is about 8.8 to 21.1, averaging 13.6.

Sodium: 65 to 210 ppm

The same report lists sodium at an average of 120 ppm. Sodium is one of the salts that adds to total dissolved solids, the measure of everything dissolved in pool water.

Why the range is so wide

A spread that large means one average cannot describe every sample on the system. Your pool's own test, not the system average, is what a technician should balance against.

Hard fill water and fierce summer evaporation work together. The sun takes the water and leaves the minerals, and the hose puts more minerals back each time you top off. Over a few seasons, calcium can build into a white crust at the tile line, rough patches on plaster and scale inside a heater. The tile cleaning page covers the waterline, and the guide on how often to drain a pool in Arizona explains when fresh water is the better fix.

Draining or backwashing a pool in Litchfield Park

The city publishes a pamphlet called "How to Drain and Backwash Pool Water Legally." It cites City Code Section 9-5-4(M), the public nuisance rule, which covers water sent onto a street, sidewalk, gutter, alley, utility easement, parking area or a neighbor's property. The city's storm drains are separate from the sanitary sewer, and anything that enters a storm drain leaves untreated.

  1. Use the sewer clean-out for a full drain. The pamphlet describes the clean-out as a threaded cap about 3 or 4 inches across and suggests consulting a plumber if you cannot find one. It warns that a wall-type clean-out is risky because water can back up into the home.
  2. Go slow. The city gives 12 gallons per minute as the maximum recommended rate, and says the safe rate may be lower depending on the drain line's size, its distance to the sewer main and the pipe's condition. It notes that most pool filter pumps push water out too fast.
  3. A small pump and a hose. The city's suggested approach is a submersible pump rated at or below 700 gallons per hour, connected to a garden hose, to empty the pool slowly.
  4. Backwash onto your own landscaping. When possible, the pamphlet says to use backwash water to irrigate your plants, and to backwash only when the filter needs it.

If you hire a company to drain the pool, ask how it will follow those steps and how fast it plans to pump. Before any drain, also check Liberty's water status. Liberty has a curtailment plan with stages, and its tariff says that in Stage 2 it may ask customers to cut water use voluntarily. Refilling a whole pool in the middle of a conservation request is something to plan around.

Fencing a pool in Litchfield Park

The city's swimming pool and spa barrier page lays out the local rules. They matter whenever a pool is built, a fence is replaced or a gate is repaired, and they matter most in a home with young children.

The barrier

A permanent, non-climbable wall or fence at least five feet tall, measured from the outside, with no opening a four-inch ball can pass through and no more than two inches of gap at the bottom.

Gates and doors

Gates swing outward from the pool, close and latch on their own, with the latch at least 54 inches above the gate bottom. If the house wall is part of the barrier, doors into the pool area need self-closing, self-latching hardware, and the city also asks for an alarm or closer on them. A pet door cannot breach the barrier.

Building a pool or spa takes a city building permit, and new fences go to the city's Design Review Board before a permit is approved. Those are construction questions for the city and a pool builder, not something the line handles. What the line can do is put you through to a pool service company for the pool you already have.

Safety first. A pool cover must be all the way off or fully secured before anyone swims. Keep children away from a pool with no fence or an open gate, and never leave a child alone near water. A green or cloudy pool is a drowning hazard because you cannot see the bottom. Never mix pool chemicals with each other; store them apart, dry and shaded, out of children's reach, and follow the label. Leave acid work to a professional. If you smell gas near a pool heater, leave the area, do not touch switches or the heater, and from a safe place call Southwest Gas at 877-860-6020 or 911.

Power, gas and monsoon season

The city's new resident page lists Arizona Public Service (APS) for electricity and Southwest Gas for natural gas, along with Liberty for water and sewer. If the pump stops after a summer storm, check the APS outage information and your own GFCI and breaker before calling anyone: reset a tripped breaker once, and if it trips again, leave it off and call.

The National Weather Service treats June 15 through September 30 as the monsoon. Storms can blow leaves, palm debris and dust into the pool in a few minutes, and heavy debris after a storm can overload baskets and the filter at the same time. Run through the safe checks below, then call if the water stays cloudy or the pump sounds wrong.

  • Baskets. Turn the pump off, then empty the skimmer and pump baskets.
  • Water level. Keep it near the middle of the skimmer opening so the pump does not pull air.
  • Filter gauge. Note the pressure reading so you can pass it on.
  • Timer. After a power blip, make sure the timer or automation still runs the pump.
  • Leaks. Look for drips at the equipment pad and note them for the company.
