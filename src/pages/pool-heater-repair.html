Pool heater repair in Phoenix is a seasonal job with a long quiet spell in between. Through most of the year nobody in the Valley needs a heater; the sun does the work. Then fall nights cool off, the heater gets switched on for the first time in months, and whatever dried out, scaled up or got packed with leaves over the summer gets tested all at once.

This page starts with gas safety, because most pool heaters here burn natural gas a few feet from where people sit. After that it explains the three kinds of heaters you will find in Phoenix backyards, what usually goes wrong with each, what you can safely check and what to ask the company you reach.

Gas safety comes before any pool heater repair

Natural gas has no smell of its own. Southwest Gas, the gas utility for much of the Valley, explains that it adds a harmless chemical called mercaptan so a leak smells like rotten eggs. If you notice that smell near the equipment pad, the heater can wait.

Do not

  • Do not touch the heater's controls, the thermostat or any electrical switch near the smell, on or off.
  • Do not smoke, strike a match or use a lighter.
  • Do not try to find the leak, open the heater cabinet or turn gas valves.
  • Do not assume a neighbor has already reported it.

Do

  • Leave the yard and the house right away, and get everyone else out too, children and pets included.
  • Once you are well away from the smell, phone 911 and then the Southwest Gas emergency line, 877-860-6020. Southwest Gas says it answers that number around the clock, for customers and non-customers.
  • Stay away until the utility or the fire department tells you it is safe.
  • Also call if you hear a hissing or roaring sound from a gas line or appliance; Southwest Gas lists that as a leak sign too.

Gas work is never a homeowner job. Pool heater repair that involves the gas supply, the gas valve or the burners belongs to a trained technician, and new or altered gas piping belongs to a contractor licensed for it.

Gas heaters, heat pumps and solar: which one is in your yard?

Before you call, it helps to know what kind of heater you have, because each one fails differently and needs a different kind of repair. Look at the equipment pad. A gas heater has a gas pipe running into it and a vent on top or on the side. A heat pump looks a bit like the outdoor unit of a home air conditioner, with a large fan and a coil, and no gas line. A solar system has panels on the roof or a rack, with pool water piped up to them and a valve that sends water up or past them.

Why heat pumps suit much of a Phoenix winter

A heat pump moves heat that is already in the air. That means it depends on the air being warm enough. The U.S. Department of Energy says heat pump pool heaters work efficiently as long as the outdoor temperature stays above roughly 45 to 50 degrees.

Phoenix winters sit mostly on the right side of that line during the day. The National Weather Service's 1991 to 2020 normals for Phoenix put the average January high at about 68 degrees and the average January low at about 46 degrees. In plain terms, a winter afternoon is comfortably warm enough for a heat pump, while the coldest hours before sunrise sit right at the edge. That is why many heat pump owners set them to run in the daytime. If a heat pump runs fine in the afternoon but struggles before dawn, that may be normal behavior rather than a fault, and it is worth asking the technician about.

Gas heaters do not care about air temperature. The Department of Energy notes that gas heaters may not be the most cost effective choice depending on your climate and how you use the pool, but they warm water quickly, which is why many Valley homes with a spa keep one.

What wears out Valley pool heaters

A few things about Phoenix are hard on pool heaters, and knowing them helps you understand what a technician finds.

Hard water and scale

The Valley's tap water is hard, meaning it carries a lot of dissolved calcium and magnesium. The City of Phoenix's 2025 water quality report lists total hardness of 172 to 302 parts per million across its system. Heat makes calcium drop out of water faster, so the hottest part of the pool system, the heat exchanger, is where scale tends to build. The Department of Energy notes that scale in the burner or heat exchanger can lower a gas heater's efficiency over time. Scale also narrows the water passages, which can lead to overheating, noise and sensor trips. Keeping the water balanced, covered on our pool cleaning and weekly maintenance page, is the best way to slow it.

Low water flow

Most pool heaters have a pressure or flow switch that keeps them from firing unless enough water is moving. A clogged filter, full baskets or a variable speed pump set to a very low speed can all keep that switch open, and the heater then shows a fault even though nothing inside it is broken. That is why a dirty filter is one of the first things to rule out. Our pool filter cleaning page explains what the pressure gauge is telling you.

Months of sitting idle

A heater that sits from spring to fall in the Phoenix heat can collect leaves, dust and nests in the cabinet and air openings. Summer monsoon storms and dust storms blow debris against the equipment pad. When the heater finally lights in the fall, that debris can block air, foul the burners or show up as a burnt smell. A quick look around the heater before the first cool night, without opening it, catches the obvious problems.

Safe checks before you call

None of these involve gas, wiring or the inside of the heater. They are the same checks a pool technician starts with, and doing them first can save a service visit for something simple.

  • Confirm the pump is running and that the timer or automation schedule gives the heater time to run.
  • Empty the skimmer and pump baskets and look at the filter pressure gauge. A reading well above its usual clean number points to a dirty filter.
  • Check the heater's own controls. It should be on, set to pool or spa as you expect, and set above the current water temperature.
  • Look for a tripped breaker or GFCI. Reset it one time only. If it trips a second time, leave it off and phone for help.
  • Write down any error code or message on the display. It helps the company you reach.
  • Look, without touching, around the heater for leaves, nests, water on the pad or soot on the cabinet.

Repair or replace the heater

Many heater problems are repairable, including the heat exchanger in some cases. The decision usually comes down to the age of the heater, which part failed and how often it has needed work.

Repair usually makes sense when

  • The fault is a sensor, switch, igniter or another replaceable part.
  • The problem was low flow, scale or debris that can be cleared.
  • The heater is fairly young and has not needed much work.
  • The cabinet, exchanger and base are in good shape.

Replacement is worth pricing when

  • The heat exchanger is cracked, burned through or badly corroded on an older unit.
  • The same fault keeps coming back after repairs.
  • Parts are hard to get for the model.
  • You heat the pool more or less often than when the heater was chosen, so a different type might fit better.

If you do replace a gas heater, efficiency has improved. The Department of Energy set a federal minimum thermal efficiency of 82 percent for gas pool heaters made on or after April 16, 2013, and says some gas pool heaters now reach 89 to 95 percent. Ask the company to explain the efficiency, size and gas supply needs of anything it proposes, and ask about a heat pump or solar if you heat the pool regularly.

Who may work on a pool heater in Arizona

Arizona's Registrar of Contractors (ROC) licenses contractors. Its R-6 Swimming Pool Service and Repair classification covers servicing pools and making minor repairs to pools and their equipment, but the ROC lists gas lines, plumbing connections to a drinking water system, gas chlorine systems and electrical work beyond the first disconnect as outside that classification. So a pool company may be able to replace a heater's igniter or sensor, while running or changing a gas line calls for a contractor licensed for gas piping. Ask which license covers the job you are being quoted.

Questions worth putting to the pool company

  • How much is the service call or diagnostic fee, and does it go toward the repair?
  • What exactly failed, and can you show me?
  • Is there scale in the heat exchanger, and what does that say about my water balance?
  • Will I get a written price before work starts?
  • If gas piping is involved, who does that part, and what is their ROC license number?
  • What is your ROC license number and classification?
