Fountain Hills: a planned town on the slopes east of the McDowells

Fountain Hills sits northeast of Scottsdale in Maricopa County. The town says McCulloch Properties, now MCO Properties, founded it in 1970 on land that had been a cattle ranch, and residents voted to incorporate in 1989. Its own background report describes the boundaries: the McDowell Mountains to the west, the Fort McDowell Indian Reservation to the east, the Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community to the south and McDowell Mountain Regional Park to the north.

The ground rises fast. That report lists elevations from about 1,520 feet in the town center to about 3,000 feet up on Golden Eagle Boulevard. Many homes sit on sloped lots with terraced yards, retaining walls and native desert left in place around them. For a pool, that means wind off open desert, blown sand and palo verde litter, and sometimes long runs of pipe between the pool and an equipment pad set lower or higher on the lot.

EPCOR water and the calcium it leaves behind

EPCOR supplies Fountain Hills through its Chaparral City district, and the town lists it as the water company for residents. EPCOR's reports describe the supply as mainly Colorado River water delivered by the Central Arizona Project (CAP), with groundwater from the local aquifer added in. Because two sources are blended, EPCOR notes that hardness and taste can change with the seasons.

Fountain Hills water hardness figures from EPCOR Chaparral City water quality reports
EPCOR reportWhat it says about hardness
2024 Chaparral City reportAbout 18 grains per gallon in the text, with a 2022 table value of 16
2021 Chaparral reportAbout 16 grains per gallon, with a 2014 reading of 16.4
Report scaleAnything above 10.5 grains per gallon is rated very hard

Hardness means calcium and magnesium dissolved in the water. EPCOR says it is not a health concern, and it does not remove it. A pool, though, works like a slow evaporating pan. Desert sun and dry wind pull water off the surface every day of the warm season, the calcium stays, and each refill brings more in. On very hard water that can mean white scale on the tile line within a season, rough plaster, and mineral buildup in heaters, salt cells and filter parts.

The fix is regular testing, not guesswork. Ask the pool company to track calcium hardness, total dissolved solids (TDS, everything dissolved in the water) and stabilizer (cyanuric acid, which shields chlorine from sunlight) along with chlorine and pH. When numbers climb too high to manage, a partial drain and refill with fresh water may be the answer. The Arizona pool draining guide walks through when that makes sense, and pool tile cleaning covers scale that has already formed.

Fountain Hills rules for drain and backwash water

The town treats pool and spa water as a non-stormwater discharge, so it must never enter the town's storm drain system. Disposal has to follow both town and Fountain Hills Sanitary District requirements.

Backwash onto your lot

For routine maintenance such as backwashing, the town says the water can go directly onto your own property, as long as it stays there until it soaks into the ground. On a sloped lot, aim it where it will not run downhill and off the property.

Full drains: yard or clean-out

Drained water must stay on your property or go into your sewer clean-out, except where the Sanitary District prohibits it. Check the tub and shower drains as soon as the pump starts, and shut it off if water backs up. A clean-out set in a wall carries more backup risk.

Never streets or washes

No pool water into the street, a storm drain or a wash. A permanent connection from the pool or spa to the clean-out is also not allowed under the swimming pool code the town has adopted.

Hillside lots, desert edges and storm season

Fountain Hills limits how much of a steep lot can be graded. Under the town's hillside ordinance, ground sloping 20 percent or more on platted residential land may only be disturbed up to 40 percent, and protected desert areas are set back from homes and walls. Some smaller-lot zoning districts are exempt. The result is many backyards where the pool sits right up against untouched desert, which is lovely to look at and a steady source of sand, leaves and seed pods in the skimmer.

That exposure shows up most during the monsoon, which the National Weather Service dates from June 15 to September 30. Dust storms and gusty thunderstorms can fill baskets in an afternoon and dump fine dirt that clouds the water and loads the filter. After a big storm, empty the baskets, check that the pump is still pulling water, and expect the pool company to brush and test. The monsoon pool care guide goes deeper, and pool filter cleaning explains why a dusty filter needs attention.

Safe checks before you call

  • Baskets. Empty the skimmer and pump baskets, especially after wind off the desert.
  • Filter gauge. Note the pressure reading. A high reading usually means the filter is loaded with dirt.
  • Water level. Check the skimmer opening. Low water lets the pump pull air.
  • Equipment pad. Look for drips or wet ground around the pump and filter, and clear away blown debris.
  • Breaker or GFCI. Reset a tripped one once. A second trip means stop: keep it off and mention it when you phone.
  • Children. Keep children away from a pool without a working barrier, and from cloudy or green water that hides the bottom.

Gas smell near a pool heater? Leave the area, keep others away and do not touch switches or the heater. From a safe place, call Southwest Gas at 877-860-6020 or 911. The town notes that Southwest Gas serves only some neighborhoods and that other homes may use propane; for a propane heater, call your propane supplier or 911. Never mix pool chemicals with each other, and store them apart, dry, shaded and out of children's reach.

Utilities and the pool barrier checklist

  • WaterEPCOR, through its Chaparral City district.
  • SewerThe Fountain Hills Sanitary District, which the town names for questions on draining into a clean-out.
  • ElectricitySRP, the provider the town lists for residents.
  • Natural gasSouthwest Gas in certain neighborhoods, including Bella Lago, Copperwynd, Eagle Mountain, FireRock and SunRidge Canyon; propane elsewhere.
  • Questions about draining or green poolsThe town lists its Code Enforcement hotline, 480-816-5193, and the Sanitary District, 480-837-9444.

For new pools, the town's pool checklist applies the International Swimming Pool and Spa Code: a barrier at least 60 inches high on the side away from the pool, small gaps at the bottom (2 inches over gravel or grass, 4 inches over concrete), and no opening wide enough for a 4 inch sphere. Gates whose release sits lower than 54 inches carry added rules. The town has since adopted the 2024 code editions by ordinance, so confirm current barrier details with its Building Safety Division before you build or replace a fence.
