Pool tile cleaning in Phoenix, AZ is mostly about one thing: the white or gray line that creeps along the tile at the water's edge. Why calcium lines form so fast in Phoenix pools

When pool water evaporates, the water leaves but the dissolved minerals stay. Every time the pool is topped off with fresh tap water, more minerals go in. Over months, the water holds more calcium than it can keep dissolved, and the extra starts to settle out as scale. The waterline is where it shows first, because the tile there is wet and then dry, again and again, as the level rises and falls.

Phoenix speeds up every part of that cycle. The Arizona Department of Water Resources says the average evaporation rate in Phoenix is about six feet of water a year, most of it in summer. A table published by the City of Scottsdale for a 400 square foot pool shows evaporation peaking at about 10.8 inches in June and dropping to about 2.6 inches in January. That is a lot of water to replace, and the replacement water is hard: the City of Phoenix's 2025 water quality report lists total hardness of 172 to 302 parts per million in its system. The city explains that this hardness comes from calcium and magnesium the source water picks up from soil on its way to the treatment plants.

Heat plays a part too. Calcium is less soluble in warm water, so hot summer water is more likely to drop scale than cool winter water.

Calcium carbonate or calcium silicate: two kinds of tile scale

Pool technicians usually sort waterline scale into two types. Which one is on your tile decides how it comes off, so it is worth asking which one the company sees.

Many Valley pools have a mix of both: a soft white film on top of older, harder deposits. A careful pool technician tests a small area before choosing a method.

How pool companies remove tile scale, and what can go wrong

There are three main approaches. Each one works on some scale and can damage some surfaces, so the right choice depends on what your tile is made of and how stubborn the buildup is.

Media blasting

A machine blows a fine media, often glass beads or another soft abrasive, against the tile at controlled pressure. It is the usual answer for thick or hard scale along a whole waterline. The water is typically lowered a few inches so the tile is dry and reachable.

Risks: media that is too coarse or pressure that is too high can etch glazed tile, pit grout or frost glass tile. Media also lands in the pool and on the deck and has to be cleaned up.

Hand work

A pumice stone, tile brush or plastic scraper used by hand on wet tile. It suits small areas, light buildup and touch ups between bigger cleanings.

Risks: pumice is softer than most glazed tile but can still scratch glossy, glass or painted tile, and it wears down grout if used hard. Metal tools and wire brushes scratch almost any tile.

Chemical descalers

Acid based or chelating products applied by a professional to soften scale, often together with hand work. They can help on calcium carbonate.

Risks: acids can etch grout, plaster and some glazes, give off fumes and throw off the pool's water balance. This is professional work, not a homeowner project.

What to expect on a tile cleaning visit

A typical job starts with a look at the tile, the grout and the type of scale, and a test on a small spot. If the method calls for it, the water level is lowered below the tile line. The City of Phoenix directs pool drain water to the sewer cleanout at the house, so ask where any water that is pumped out will go. After the cleaning, the pool is refilled to its normal level and the water is tested, because fresh fill water and any cleaning products change the balance.

Cleaning sometimes uncovers loose, cracked or missing tiles and crumbling grout that the scale was hiding. Those are repairs, not cleaning. A cracked tile line can also be a sign of movement or a leak, which is covered on our pool leak and crack repair page and the general pool repair page.

Muriatic acid and melamine sponges: the safe answers

Muriatic acid on pool tile

Muriatic acid is a form of hydrochloric acid. It is corrosive, it releases fumes that hurt eyes and lungs, and it can etch grout, plaster and some tile finishes. It also lowers the pool's pH and alkalinity if it drips in. This page does not give acid instructions, on purpose. Leave acid work to a pool professional.

Never mix pool chemicals with each other, and never combine acid with chlorine products, which can release toxic gas. Store pool chemicals apart, dry, shaded and out of children's reach, and follow every label.

Melamine sponges on pool tile

A melamine foam sponge is a mild abrasive. It can lift light film and early spotting from smooth glazed tile at the waterline, which makes it a reasonable tool for routine touch ups. It will not remove hard scale, and heavy scrubbing can dull glossy or glass tile.

Test a small spot you do not usually see first. Use it only on light film, rinse the area and stop if the finish looks different.

How often to clean pool tile, and how to slow the scale down

Light film should come off as part of regular cleaning, whether you do it or a weekly service does. A deeper descaling is needed when the band is visible and brushing no longer moves it. Some Valley pools need that every year or two; others go longer. The real answer is to stop the scale forming in the first place.

Keep the water balanced

Scale forms when the water is more saturated with calcium than it can hold. Pool professionals measure this with the Langelier Saturation Index (LSI), a single number built from pH, total alkalinity, calcium hardness, water temperature and total dissolved solids. A reading near zero means the water is balanced. A positive reading means the water tends to leave scale behind; a negative reading means it tends to eat at plaster and grout. Because water temperature is part of the formula, water that is balanced in March can turn scale forming in July. A good weekly service tests for this and adjusts with the seasons. Our pool cleaning and weekly maintenance page covers what that service includes.

  • Test the water regularly with a home test kit and keep pH from drifting high.
  • Brush the waterline when you brush the pool, before film hardens.
  • Watch the water level. Keep it steady at the middle of the skimmer opening so the same strip of tile is not wetting and drying all day.
  • Use a cover when the pool is not in use. The Arizona Department of Water Resources says pool covers can cut evaporation by 90 to 95 percent, and less evaporation means less top off water and fewer added minerals.
  • Ask about high mineral water. When calcium and dissolved solids climb too high, the long term fix is replacing some or all of the water. The City of Phoenix says draining should be infrequent; our guide to how often to drain a pool in Arizona explains the choices.
