Paradise Valley: a town built around the one acre lot

Paradise Valley sits between Phoenix and Scottsdale in Maricopa County. Its own history says the area was rural and residential before incorporation, with modest homes on parcels of one to five acres, and that residents incorporated on May 24, 1961, with about 2,000 people, mainly to keep larger neighbors from annexing them. Their stated goals were one house per acre, an all residential town and light government. The town gives its 2020 Census population as 12,658.

That plan still shapes backyards. The standard R-43 zoning district is built around a minimum lot of 43,560 square feet, which is one acre. Pools here tend to have room around them: long decks, lawns, mature trees and planting beds. More trees and more yard mean more leaves, pods and blossoms in the water, so skimmer baskets and filters fill faster than they would behind a small tract home.

Which water fills your pool depends on your address

Most Valley cities run one water utility. Paradise Valley does not. The town's FAQ and utility page name three providers inside town limits: Berneil Water Company, City of Phoenix Water Services and EPCOR. Its water companies page also lists the City of Scottsdale, so the town's own pages do not agree on the count. The town says where you live decides which one serves you, and it posts a provider boundary map.

That matters for a pool because each system has its own source water and its own hardness. Hardness is the calcium and magnesium dissolved in water. EPCOR's Paradise Valley system is one of the few with published figures that apply directly here:

  • SourceEPCOR's 2024 report says the system draws groundwater from wells in the West Salt River Valley sub-basin and serves about 5,300 connections.
  • ArsenicThe report shows arsenic results under the federal limit of 10 parts per billion.
  • BackupThe system has an interconnect with Phoenix, fed by the 24th Street treatment plant, which treats water from the Central Arizona Project (CAP) and Salt River Project canals.
  • HardnessAbout 12 grains per gallon in the 2024 report. EPCOR says hardness is not a health concern.

In a pool, those minerals only go one way. Summer heat pulls water off the surface, the calcium stays, and each top-up adds more. Over time that shows up as a white crust along the tile, rough plaster and scale inside a heater or salt cell. If you are on Berneil, Phoenix or Scottsdale water, ask your provider for its latest water quality report, and ask the pool company to test calcium hardness and total dissolved solids (TDS, the sum of everything dissolved in the water) at your address rather than guessing. The pool tile cleaning page explains how scale comes off, and the guide to draining a pool in Arizona covers when a partial drain makes sense.

Draining a pool in Paradise Valley: sewer, septic or yard

The town says chlorinated pool water going to a street, storm drain, wash or a neighbor's property breaks local, state and federal rules. Where it can go depends on how your house handles wastewater.

Homes on the sewer

The town's pool draining page says the Town Code now allows draining to the sanitary sewer through the clean-out, usually a four inch cap between the house and the main. Pump slowly, since too much flow can back up the line, and never leave a permanent pipe connected.

Homes on septic

The town's FAQ says many properties are on septic, not sewer. Do not drain a pool into a septic clean-out: the town warns it can overwhelm the tank and cause it to fail. Use your own retention basin, landscape irrigation, or a hauling company instead.

Backwash on your own lot

The town's storm drainage manual bars draining or backwashing into a street, storm drain or natural wash. New home plans must show where backwash water goes, or use a cartridge filter that does not need backwashing.

Confirm before you drain

One caution: an older town handout on swimming pool drainage says pool water may not go to the sewer at all, and the town still posts that handout online. The newer pool draining page is the more recent word, but call the town's Engineering Department, listed on that page at (480) 348-3681, before a full drain. Sewer service is also split: the town names two sewer providers, the City of Phoenix and the town itself, and says the City of Scottsdale operates and maintains the town's sewer. A pool company that offers to drain your pool should tell you exactly where the water will go.

Power is split too. The town says both APS and SRP serve Paradise Valley and publishes a map of their service areas, so the utility named on your bill is the one to call about an outage that stops the pump.

Drains and refills are often tied to a calcium problem. Before you schedule one, ask the company for its hardness and TDS readings, so you know the fresh water will actually help. The repair or resurface guide is worth a look if the plaster is rough or stained, since a resurface job usually means draining anyway.

Safe checks before you call

  • Skimmer and pump baskets. On a treed lot, empty them often, and always after wind. A packed basket starves the pump.
  • Filter gauge. Write down the pressure reading. Well above normal usually points to a dirty filter.
  • Timer or automation. Check the run schedule after any power outage.
  • Water level. Look at the skimmer opening. If the level falls fast, mention it, since it may be more than evaporation.
  • Breaker or GFCI. Reset a tripped one once. If it trips again, leave it off and tell the company.
  • Children. Keep children away from any pool without a working barrier, and away from cloudy or green water where the bottom cannot be seen.

Gas smell near the pool heater? Leave the area, keep others away and do not touch the heater, switches or controls. From a safe place, call Southwest Gas at 877-860-6020 or 911. Never mix pool chemicals with each other; store them apart, dry, shaded and out of reach of children.

The Paradise Valley pool code: barriers, hillsides and rentals

People search for the "Paradise Valley pool code" mostly to learn the fence rules. Here is what the town publishes, in plain terms. The town's handouts are dated in places, so treat this as a starting point and confirm with the Building Department before you build or change anything.

  • When an enclosure is requiredThe resident guide says new pools and other bodies of water 18 inches deep or more must be protected by an enclosure that meets the Town Code.
  • Recommended heightThe same guide recommends a five foot barrier above grade, measured on the side facing away from the pool, for new and existing pools.
  • Hillside lotsThe hillside rules generally prohibit fence walls but make an exception for pool barrier view fences, along with retaining walls and view guard rails.
  • PermitsThe town's permit page lists a pool and spa permit with a site plan, structural calculations and an engineer's pool plan, plus a barrier requirements handout.

The town's hillside rules cover lots on the slopes of Camelback Mountain, Mummy Mountain and the Phoenix Mountains. On those slopes, the pool is often tucked into a terraced yard close to native desert, so expect more blown sand and plant debris after storms.

Short-term rentals add one more layer. The town's rental article requires the owner to make sure any pool at the rental meets the town's barrier requirements, with fines that climb for repeat violations. If you rent out a home with a pool, ask the pool company to note gate latches and barrier issues it sees on each visit.
