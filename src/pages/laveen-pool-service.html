Laveen: farmers, a river crossing and a village of Phoenix

Farmers and dairymen started settling the Laveen area in 1884. For decades the Salt River, which ran year round until Roosevelt Dam was finished in 1911, cut the area off from Phoenix; the nearest bridge was at Central Avenue, more than six miles away. A school named Laveen School opened in 1913, and in March 1918 Walter Laveen became the first postmaster, running the post office from the back of his family's general store near what is now 51st Avenue and Dobbins Road. Alfalfa, cotton and dairy farms carried the area for most of the next century.

The City of Phoenix describes Laveen Village as sitting between South Mountain and the Salt River, and its planning for the village leans on keeping some of that rural feel while new housing fills in. A city fact sheet projects about 70,450 residents for the village by 2030. In practice that means a pool on a fresh lot next to a field, or a pool behind a house that has stood for decades, a short drive apart.

City of Phoenix or Maricopa County: why it matters for a Laveen pool

Part of Laveen is a Phoenix urban village, and part is unincorporated county land. Which one you live in changes a few things the pool company will ask about.

Who supplies the water

Inside Phoenix, the city's water department is your provider, and its annual report spells out hardness and dissolved solids. In the county part, your provider may be different. The name on your water bill tells you whose report to read.

Whose rules apply

Rules on permits and on where drained or backwashed pool water may go come from the city inside its limits and from the county outside them. Ask the pool company which set applies to your address before it drains anything.

Who you call for city services

Laveen Village is split between two Phoenix City Council districts, 7 and 8. Residents of the county part deal with Maricopa County instead. Neither one services pools, but knowing which applies saves a wasted phone call.

Where a pool came with a newer house, the first questions are about routine care: setting up weekly cleaning and maintenance, keeping dust out of the filter, and getting a pool filter cleaning on a sensible schedule. When a pool gets away from its owner in summer, green pool cleanup is the usual call. Older properties bring pool pump repair into it, since a pump that has run every day for years eventually starts to hum, leak or trip the breaker.

Dust, fields and hard water in a Laveen backyard

Monsoon season, as the National Weather Service defines it, runs from June 15 through September 30. The afternoon storms often arrive with a front of blowing dust, and an area that still mixes working fields and graded lots with its neighborhoods has plenty of loose ground for the wind to lift. What lands in the pool is fine silt, leaves and seed fluff. It clouds the water, eats chlorine and packs the filter, which is why a Laveen pool can need its filter cleaned more often than the calendar suggests.

The water coming out of the hose adds its own load. For homes on City of Phoenix water, the 2025 report lists total hardness from 172 to 302 milligrams per liter and total dissolved solids from 464 to 716 parts per million. Evaporation takes water out of a pool all summer and leaves those minerals in it. Over time calcium hardness creeps up, scale forms along the tile, and the water gets harder to balance. A pool technician who tests calcium and stabilizer as well as chlorine and pH can tell you when the numbers call for a partial drain and refill.

Before you call: quick, safe checks

  • Look at the water level. After a hot, windy week it can drop below the skimmer and leave the pump sucking air.
  • Clear the baskets. Field dust and leaves collect in the skimmer and pump baskets first.
  • Read the filter pressure gauge. Write down the number. A reading well above the usual clean pressure means the filter is loaded.
  • Check the clock. Storm outages can reset a pool timer, leaving the pump running at night and off all day.
  • Test with a home kit. Chlorine and pH readings help the company understand what it is walking into.

Keep it safe. If the water turns green or murky, keep children out and away, because the bottom of the pool cannot be seen. Never mix pool chemicals together, and keep them stored separately in a dry, shaded spot out of reach. A pool heater that smells of gas means leave the area, leave the heater and switches alone, and call Southwest Gas or 911 from a safe distance.

A freeway through the middle, and new pools behind new houses

The Loop 202 South Mountain Freeway, approved by Maricopa County voters in 1985 and again in 2004, opened in full at the end of 2019. It runs south of South Mountain, through Laveen, and up to Interstate 10 at 59th Avenue. A Laveen transportation study posted by the city describes the freeway as cutting right across the community.

A pool that came with a newly built home has its own short list of questions. Find the builder's paperwork and any equipment warranty before you pay for a repair, since a fault in the first year or two may be covered. Ask the pool company to look at how the deck and yard drain after a storm, because rain off a fresh lot can carry soil straight into the water. And ask for the company's Arizona Registrar of Contractors license number for anything beyond routine cleaning.
