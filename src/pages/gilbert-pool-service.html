Gilbert: a railroad siding that grew into a town of more than 260,000

Gilbert started in 1902, when William "Bobby" Gilbert gave land for a rail siding on the Arizona Eastern Railway's line between Phoenix and Florence. The town incorporated on July 6, 1920, and until the late 1920s it was known as the "Hay Capital of the World." Fifty years later it was still tiny, with 1,971 residents in 1970. During the 1970s the Town Council approved a strip annexation of 53 square miles of county land, which set the shape of the Gilbert you see today.

The houses came later and came fast. The census counted 208,453 people in 2010 and 267,918 in 2020, and housing units rose from 74,907 to 93,230 over that decade. The town describes itself as the largest town in the United States and expects to reach build out within about a decade. Many of the pools built during those boom years are now old enough to need a new pump or a first resurfacing.

Where a Gilbert pool's water comes from

The town publishes the mix behind its supply. Each part shows up in the pool in a different way.

Colorado River through the CAP, about 41 percent

Central Arizona Project water is treated at the Santan Vista treatment plant, which Gilbert shares with Chandler.

Salt and Verde rivers through SRP, about 40 percent

Salt River Project water is treated at the town's North Water Treatment Plant. The town is rebuilding that plant in a project scheduled to finish by winter 2028, after which it is expected to supply about 70 percent of Gilbert's water.

Reclaimed water, about 15 percent

Treated wastewater is used for landscape irrigation and other non-drinking uses, not sent to your tap.

Groundwater, about 4 percent

A small share comes from wells. Because the blend changes through the year, the town's yearly water quality reports are the best place to see what your tap delivered.

What Gilbert's water does to a pool

Gilbert's water department says the town's water averages 8 to 10 grains per gallon of hardness and suggests setting a softener at 10 to 12 grains to cover seasonal swings. Hardness is the dissolved calcium and magnesium in the water. That is moderate by Valley standards, but a pool does not stay at tap level for long. In summer the sun pulls water out of the pool every day, the minerals stay put, and each top off adds more. After a few seasons the calcium in a Gilbert pool can be far above what came out of the hose.

High calcium plus warm water and a pH that drifts high is the recipe for scale: a white or gray crust along the tile line, rough spots on plaster, and mineral buildup inside a heater or on a salt cell's plates. A careful pool technician tests calcium hardness and total alkalinity, not just chlorine and pH, and uses a saturation index (LSI, a single number that says whether water is likely to scale or to etch plaster) to decide what to adjust. Ask the company you are put through to how often it checks calcium and what it suggests when the reading keeps rising.

Draining a pool or backwashing a filter in Gilbert

Gilbert does not require a permit to drain a pool, but it does rank where the water may go. If your neighborhood has an HOA, its own draining policy applies on top of the town's.

  1. Your own yard comes first. Spread the water over landscaping or other ground that can soak it up. None of it may run off into a ditch, the street, a storm drain or a retention area, or flood a neighbor.
  2. The sewer cleanout is next. If the lot has no room, use the sanitary sewer cleanout. This is also the route for heavily treated water and for saltwater pools. If there are two cleanouts, use the one closest to the house, and watch the shower and tub drains for backup once the pump starts.
  3. The street is the last choice, with limits. A saltwater pool may never go to the street, and the town's guidance flags lake communities and streets with flooding problems. Water full of sediment belongs in the yard or the cleanout instead, and a permanent pipe from the pump to the cleanout or the curb violates town code.

Filter backwash has a stricter rule: it may never go into the gutter or the street. Rinse it onto grass or gravel on your own property, or catch it in a tub, let the solids settle, pour the water into the sewer and put the dried solids in the trash. To report pool water running into a public street, the town lists Gilbert Code Compliance at (480) 503-6879. How often a drain is even worth it is covered in how often to drain a pool in Arizona.

Pool fences, power and rebates in Gilbert

Pool enclosures

A Gilbert code compliance FAQ quotes the Land Development Code: every pool must be enclosed by the walls of the house or by a solid wall, chain link fence or wrought iron fence at least 5 feet and no more than 6 feet tall. Gates should be about the same height, self-closing and self-latching, and no opening may let a 4 inch sphere through. The town notes a fence may need a permit, and its code pages have been renumbered over time, so confirm the current rule with Development Services before you replace a gate. Gilbert Fire and Rescue adds practical advice: keep chairs and tables away from the fence so a child cannot push them over and climb in.

A fence is a backup, not a babysitter. Never leave a child alone near water, and treat a green or cloudy pool as dangerous because no one can see the bottom. If yours has turned, read about green pool cleanup.

Electricity and gas

Electric service in Gilbert comes from SRP or APS depending on the address. Southwest Gas supplies natural gas. Neither utility touches pool equipment: if the lights work but the pump is silent, the problem is in the pool's equipment or its circuit.

Water conservation rebates

Gilbert's residential grass removal rebate pays up to $2,000, with up to $1,000 more through an extra incentive, but you need pre-approval before you start, and pools and fountains may not be built in the converted area. The town also lists a rebate of up to $250 for a smart irrigation controller. Funding is limited, so check the current terms with Gilbert Water Conservation.
