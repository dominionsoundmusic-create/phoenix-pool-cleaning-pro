How often to clean a pool filter is the question every new pool owner in the Valley asks, and the honest answer is: when the gauge tells you. A filter in a quiet January backyard can go a long time between cleanings. The same filter in July, with kids in the water every afternoon and a dust storm rolling through, may need attention within days. This guide gives you the pressure rule, a season-by-season schedule for Phoenix, and the things that shorten the wait.

The pressure rule behind every pool filter cleaning schedule

Your filter has a pressure gauge, usually on top of the tank. As the filter holds more dirt, water has a harder time getting through and the needle climbs. Pentair's filter manual describes exactly that, and adds that the rate of buildup changes with how many people swim, the water temperature and the weather.

The rule of thumb comes from the filter makers. Hayward's knowledge base says to backwash or clean when pressure rises 8 to 10 psi over the clean starting pressure. Its cartridge filter manuals use 7 to 10 psi, or a drop in flow below what you need. Both point to the same habit: know your clean number, then watch for the rise.

Keep a simple pressure log

  1. Write down the clean number. Right after a cleaning, with the pump at its usual speed, note the reading. A strip of tape on the tank or a note on your phone works.
  2. Read the gauge at the same pump speed each time. Variable-speed pumps show different pressures at different speeds, so compare like with like.
  3. Check once a week, and after storms. A weekly glance shows the trend; a check after a haboob or heavy rain catches a sudden jump.
  4. Act at the rise, not at a date. When the reading sits about 8 to 10 psi over the clean number, it is time.
  5. Watch the clean number itself. If it creeps higher after each cleaning, the media may be wearing out or the plumbing may need a look.

A falling reading is a different signal. Pressure lower than your clean number usually means the pump is not getting enough water, often because a basket is full or the water level is low. The pool filter cleaning page has a symptom table for low and high pressure and what a professional clean involves for each filter type.

When to clean a pool filter cartridge versus DE or sand

The pressure rule applies to all three filters, but each type reaches it differently and is cleaned differently.

Cartridge

Pool and Spa News notes that many residential cartridge filters are built with 300 to 600 square feet of pleated media, enough that some pools need a full cleaning only once or twice a year. Others, with smaller filters or heavy use, need it every few weeks. HomeGuide's cost guide suggests hosing cartridges every few weeks and replacing them every two to three years. That spread is exactly why the gauge matters more than any number of weeks.

DE

A DE filter is backwashed when pressure rises, then given fresh powder. Backwashing does not remove everything, so the grids still need to be taken out and cleaned from time to time. A pool technician can tell you how often your filter's grids need that fuller cleaning, based on how fast the pressure returns after a backwash. Spent DE has to be handled and disposed of under your city's rules.

Sand

A sand filter is backwashed at the rise. The sand itself wears out over years, not weeks. Pool and Spa News says lightly used pools can go about five years before new sand is needed and busy pools about three, and Hayward suggests replacing it when backwashing stops bringing the pressure down.

Backwash water and cartridge rinse water carry dirt and chlorine, and Valley cities restrict where they may go. In Phoenix, the Water Services Department asks residents to keep pool water out of the street and alley, and to use the sewer cleanout or their own landscaping. Other cities have their own rules, collected in the guide to draining a pool in Arizona.

What shortens the time between cleanings

If your filter needs cleaning far more often than your neighbor's, one of these is usually the reason:

  • A dust storm or heavy rain. Dirt, leaves and runoff from the deck go straight to the skimmer and the filter.
  • Algae. Even a faint green tint loads a filter fast, and clearing a green pool can mean cleaning the filter several times in a few days. See why pools turn green.
  • New plaster. Pentair notes that plaster dust from a new or resurfaced finish can make filter runs very short until it is gone.
  • Heavy swimming. Sunscreen, lotions and body oils coat filter media. A busy summer weekend shows up on the gauge.
  • Hard water and scale. City of Phoenix water is hard, with total hardness of roughly 10 to 17.6 grains per gallon in its 2025 report, and minerals can build up in filter fabric over time.
  • A filter too small for the pump. Hayward notes that pressure that stays high with weak flow can mean the pump moves more water than the filter is built for.

Some habits lengthen the time between cleanings. Empty the skimmer and pump baskets often, so leaves never reach the filter. Brush and skim before vacuuming so the coarse debris comes out in the net. Keep the chlorine steady: the CDC recommends at least 1 part per million of free chlorine in a home pool, or at least 2 ppm if you use stabilized chlorine, and a pH of 7.0 to 7.8. Water that is properly sanitized does not grow the algae that clogs a filter. Test with a home kit and follow the product labels. Never mix pool chemicals with each other, store them apart in a dry, shaded spot out of children's reach, and leave acid work to a professional.

Signs it is time now, whatever the schedule says

  • The gauge reads about 8 to 10 psi over your clean number.
  • The return jets push noticeably less water than usual.
  • The water looks dull or hazy even though chlorine tests in range.
  • A dust storm, heavy rain or a burst of leaves just went through.
  • You are finishing a green pool recovery or the pool was just replastered.

Leave the filter closed if you are unsure. Never open a filter lid or clamp with the pump running. Turn the pump off and release the pressure at the air relief valve first. A breaker or GFCI that trips can be reset once; if it trips again, stop and call a pool technician. Keep children away from the equipment pad and from any pool whose bottom you cannot see.

Letting a pool company keep the schedule

Many weekly pool service plans in the Valley include filter cleaning at set intervals, while others charge for it as a separate visit. When you talk to a company, ask how often the filter is cleaned under the plan, whether that changes in monsoon season, and whether they will tell you the before and after pressure.
