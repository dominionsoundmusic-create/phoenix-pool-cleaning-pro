Queen Creek: from cotton fields to subdivisions in two counties

Before it was Queen Creek, the area was Rittenhouse, named for a railroad spur near what are now Rittenhouse and Ellsworth roads. Farming shaped the area for decades, and in 1928 the Rittenhouse property was sold to Leo Ellsworth, whose Ellsworth Brothers Farms grew cotton and large acreages of produce and ran cattle, sheep and a dairy herd.

The town did not incorporate until September 5, 1989. Growth since then has been fast, and the population more than doubled between the 2010 and 2020 censuses. Queen Creek lies in both Maricopa and Pinal counties, so the county on your tax bill depends on your street. Because so much was built in recent years, many Queen Creek pools are still fairly new.

Pool service in Queen Creek: what calls are usually about

Weekly pool cleaning and maintenance is the most common request: in a town where new construction and working farm fields sit close to neighborhoods, dust and plant debris find their way into the water. Hard well water makes pool tile cleaning a regular need. When a pump or heater fails, a pool repair visit finds the cause, and a skipped week in summer can turn into a green pool cleanup.

Queen Creek's water: mostly wells, a little Colorado River

Queen Creek runs its own water utility, and its supply leans on groundwater far more than on river water.

Wells across town

The town's customers hold a 100 year assured water supply, but that supply depends on groundwater pumped from wells, which is why hardness varies from place to place.

A share of the Colorado River

Through a water transfer from the Cibola Valley, the town receives 2,033 acre feet of Colorado River water a year, delivered through the Central Arizona Project (CAP) canal along its eastern edge. The town puts its reliance on the river at about 10 percent.

Replenishing what is pumped

Under Arizona's 1980 Groundwater Management Act, the town's pumped groundwater must be replenished. Most customers, 89 percent, belong to the Central Arizona Groundwater Replenishment District, and the town uses all its treated wastewater to offset pumping.

Hard well water and a Queen Creek pool

The Town of Queen Creek says its water runs from 6 to 17 grains per gallon of hardness, the calcium and magnesium dissolved in it. That is a wide spread, and it reflects water drawn from many wells rather than one plant. Two homes a few miles apart can fill their pools with noticeably different water, which is a good reason to have the pool tested rather than guess.

Evaporation does the rest. On a hot, dry day a pool loses water to the air while the minerals stay behind, and every refill adds more. As calcium climbs and the water warms, it starts to come out as scale: a pale crust at the waterline, gritty patches on the plaster, and deposits inside heaters and on salt cell plates. A careful pool technician checks calcium hardness, pH and total alkalinity together and adjusts toward balance, using a saturation index (LSI) that predicts whether water will leave scale or eat at plaster. Cyanuric acid, the stabilizer that shields chlorine from the sun, gets tested too, because too little lets chlorine burn off by afternoon and too much makes it sluggish.

Dust is the other constant. The National Weather Service treats June 15 through September 30 as monsoon season, and in a typical year one to three dust storms push into the Phoenix area, most of them from the southeast, and Queen Creek sits on the southeast edge of the metro. A storm can drop silt and leaves into the pool, clog the filter and eat through the chlorine in an evening. The monsoon pool care guide explains how to handle the morning after.

Before you call: safe things to look at

  • Skimmer and pump baskets. Turn the pump off, then clear leaves and debris. A full basket starves the pump of water.
  • Filter pressure. Write down the gauge reading. A reading well above the clean number points to a dirty filter.
  • Water level. It should reach about the middle of the skimmer opening. Note if it drops faster than evaporation would explain.
  • The equipment pad. Look for drips, wet soil or a pump that hums without spinning, and tell the company what you see.
  • Tripped GFCI or breaker. Reset it once. If it trips again, leave it off for a professional.

Keep it safe. Never mix pool chemicals, store them apart in a cool, dry spot out of children's reach, and follow the label. Leave acid work to a professional. The town lists Southwest Gas for natural gas, and some homes use propane instead. If you smell gas near a pool heater, get away from it without flipping switches or touching the heater, then from a safe distance call Southwest Gas at 877-860-6020 (or your propane supplier) or 911.

Queen Creek's pool draining policy

Queen Creek's policy sets out three ways to get rid of pool water, in order of preference. It also asks you to backwash only until the water runs clear and to drain a pool only when it is really necessary.

1. Your own property

Drain all or part of the water, including backwash, onto your own lot, ideally onto lawn or landscaping. Do not let it run onto a neighbor's property, the street, an alley or other town right of way.

2. The sewer clean-out

Use the sewer clean-out if the lot cannot take it all, the pool is salt, green, freshly shocked or acid washed, or you are only backwashing. The town has advised against this route for homes on a septic tank.

3. The curb, with conditions

Water may go to the curb and on to the neighborhood retention basin only if it is de-chlorinated, your street does not usually flood, the hose reaches the curb and nothing floods next door.

The town says no permit is required to drain at this time. Queen Creek also has a sewer billing quirk that touches pools: residential sewer fees are figured from water use over three winter billing cycles, roughly mid-November to mid-February. Fill a new or drained pool in that window and the next year's sewer charge can rise, so the town suggests filling at another time. If you already did, a pool fill can count as a qualifying event for an adjustment. For when a drain makes sense at all, read how often to drain a pool in Arizona.

Queen Creek pool barrier requirements, power and gas

The barrier rule

Queen Creek requires a barrier for every swimming pool, above ground or in-ground, under Ordinance No. 479-10, which took effect on June 19, 2010. The town gives two ways to meet it. One is a 5 foot fence on three sides of the pool paired with either a motorized safety pool cover, a ground level door that closes and latches by itself, or a 4 foot barrier between the pool and the living quarters. The other is a 5 foot fence on every side. Pools and spas are on the town's list of projects that need a permit, and the town publishes separate fencing requirements. The full details are in the ordinance, so confirm them with Building Safety.

Queen Creek Fire and Medical adds that pool fences should be five feet high and gates should close and latch on their own. No barrier replaces an adult's eyes: never leave a child unsupervised near water, and keep everyone out of a cloudy or green pool, where the bottom cannot be seen.

Electricity, gas and water service

The town's resident resources list SRP as the electric provider, with outages reported at 602-236-8888, and Southwest Gas for natural gas. Queen Creek's water utility also reaches past the town line: when it bought the H2O water system in 2013, its service area grew east into unincorporated Pinal County, and in 2023 the town took over wastewater service for the Ironwood Crossing and Encanterra communities. If a pump stops while the house has power, the fault is in the pool's equipment or circuit, and that is a job for pool equipment repair.
