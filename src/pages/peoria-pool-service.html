Peoria: a city that keeps building north

Peoria stretches far to the north of its original town site, and much of its newer housing is in the north. Water planning there has followed that growth. Since the late 1990s, Peoria and Glendale have shared Colorado River water from the Central Arizona Project, and they jointly own the Pyramid Peak water treatment plant in north Phoenix. Glendale holds the larger share and operates it. The plant serves the northern parts of both cities, including Peoria's Vistancia area.

As new neighborhoods kept coming, the city looked to expand the plant to keep pace with residential growth. For a pool owner, that history means your tap water depends on where you live. Peoria's yearly water quality report lists the sources and the hardness of the water reaching homes, and it is worth reading before anyone tells you how hard your water is.

An older Peoria pool and a newer one need different things

The same street rules apply to both: hard water, high heat and summer dust. What wears out first is different.

Older pools in the south end

Expect worn plaster, tile with years of scale on it, and equipment that has been patched more than once. A careful pool technician will look at the age of the pump and filter before suggesting another repair, and will tell you if the surface is near the point where repair or resurfacing is the real question.

Newer pools up north

New plaster needs careful water balance while it cures, and newer pads often carry salt cells, variable-speed pumps and automation. Keep the builder's paperwork handy. A fault in the first years may still be covered, so check that paperwork first, before any money changes hands.

Pool service in Peoria, AZ: what to ask before you agree to a plan

Every company sets its own plan and price, and the line does not quote either. These questions make two quotes easier to compare.

  • What does each weekly visit include? Skimming, brushing, vacuuming, baskets and water testing are the core. Ask how often the filter gets cleaned.
  • Do you test calcium hardness and stabilizer? Cyanuric acid (stabilizer) shields chlorine from the sun, but too much of it slows chlorine down. Both numbers matter in the desert.
  • Are chemicals in the monthly price or billed separately? Get the answer in writing.
  • What is your Arizona ROC license number? Needed for repair, equipment and resurfacing work. Look it up yourself.
  • Does my HOA have rules that affect the job? If you live in a master-planned community, check its rules on equipment changes, noise or where water may drain, and share them.

Safe checks before you call

  • Listen to the pump. Humming without spinning, grinding or screeching are all worth describing on the call. Turn it off if it is screeching.
  • Look at the pad. Note any drip or puddle at the pump, filter or heater, and clear leaves and debris away from the equipment.
  • Check the automation. A schedule can reset after an outage. Make sure the pump is set to run.
  • Test the water. A home test kit reading for chlorine and pH helps the company know what it is walking into.

Safety first. Never mix pool chemicals with each other, and keep them stored apart, dry, shaded and away from children. Do not open up motors or wiring. If a breaker trips a second time, leave it off. Never leave a child alone near the water, and keep a gate or cover on a pool that is not fenced.
