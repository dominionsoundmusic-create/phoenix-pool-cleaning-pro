Chandler: a farm town that turned into a chip-making city

Chandler was founded in 1912 and incorporated on February 16, 1920. For decades it stayed small, with only about 3,800 residents in 1950, even after Williams Air Force Base opened nearby in 1941. Then the farm economy gave way to electronics and computer chip plants, and the population took off: about 30,000 in 1980, 90,769 in 1990, 176,240 in 2000 and 275,987 in the 2020 census.

That timeline tells you a lot about the pools. A city that grew sixfold in twenty years built most of its homes, and most of its backyard pools, in a short window. Many of those pools now have original plumbing and plaster and their second or third pump. When you call about a Chandler pool, it helps to know roughly when the house was built, because the age of the finish and equipment shapes what a pool technician looks for first.

Chandler pool service and maintenance people call about

Most Chandler calls fall into four groups. Routine pool cleaning and weekly maintenance keeps chlorine up and dust off the floor through a long, hot season. Pool tile cleaning deals with the white crust that hard water leaves at the waterline. A dirty filter is often the first casualty of a dust storm, which is what pool filter cleaning covers. Three rivers and a well field in every glass

Chandler does not lean on a single source. The city's water supply pages describe a blend, and each part leaves its mark in the pool.

Salt and Verde rivers

About 57 percent of Chandler's drinking water comes from the Salt and Verde rivers, delivered by the Salt River Project's dams, reservoirs and canals. The city's own surface water plant draws it from the Consolidated Canal.

The Colorado River

About 37 percent is Colorado River water, carried across the desert in the Central Arizona Project (CAP) canal. Chandler also shares a treatment plant, Santan Vista, with the Town of Gilbert.

Groundwater and stored water

About 6 percent comes from city wells. Chandler has banked surface water underground and built recharge basins and injection wells so it can pump stored water during a shortage.

What 16.5 grains of hardness means for a Chandler pool

Hardness is the calcium and magnesium dissolved in water. Chandler's water quality FAQ puts its tap water anywhere from about 5 to 20 grains per gallon depending on the source in use, with a citywide average of 16.5. The city's 2023 water quality report listed total hardness of 163 to 340 milligrams per liter, which it equated to 9.5 to 19.9 grains. The same FAQ notes that the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency has set no limit for hardness, which is not considered a health concern. For a pool, though, it matters a lot.

Here is why. A Chandler pool can lose a lot of water to evaporation in summer, and only the water leaves. The calcium stays behind, so every top off adds more minerals to a pool that already had plenty. When the water also runs warm and slightly high in pH, that calcium comes out of solution and sticks to the warmest, roughest surfaces: the tile line, the heat exchanger inside a gas heater, and the plates of a salt cell. Pool technicians use a measure called the saturation index (LSI) to judge whether water is likely to scale or to etch plaster, and they adjust pH and alkalinity to keep it near balance.

Because the source blend shifts, a Chandler pool filled in one season can test differently from the same pool refilled in another. That is a good reason to ask the company you are put through to how often it tests calcium hardness, not just chlorine and pH, and what it does when the number keeps climbing. Sometimes the answer is partly draining and refilling, which brings the city's drain rules into play.

Safe checks before you call

  • Baskets. Empty the skimmer and pump baskets, especially after wind. With the pump off, a packed basket is the most common reason for weak flow.
  • Filter gauge. Note the pressure reading and compare it with what it reads when the filter is clean. A big rise means the filter needs attention.
  • Water level. The water should sit about halfway up the skimmer opening. Too low and the pump can pull air.
  • Timer. Check that the timer or automation still has the right time after a power blip.
  • Breaker or GFCI. Reset a tripped one once. If it trips again, leave it off and tell the company.

Chemicals and gas, safely. Never mix pool chemicals with each other. Store them apart in a dry, shaded spot out of children's reach and follow the label. If you smell gas near a pool heater, leave the area, do not touch switches or the heater, and from a safe distance call the Southwest Gas emergency number on your bill or 911.

Draining or backwashing a pool in Chandler

Chandler's stormwater rules are about keeping pool water out of the street. Storm drains carry water away untreated, and chlorinated, salty or dirty pool water is not supposed to reach them. The city's pool drainage guidance gives Chandler homeowners and pool companies clear lines to follow.

What Chandler allows

  • Draining into the sanitary sewer cleanout on your own property, with no permit needed.
  • Keeping backwash and drain water on your own landscaping.
  • Refilling without notifying the city, though your water bill will show it.

What Chandler prohibits

  • Pipes or hoses that run off your lot to the street, alley or other city right of way.
  • Treating that line as a drain path: the city calls it a direct connection to the storm drain system.
  • Street draining without asking: if you cannot find your cleanout, request an exception first.

The cleanout is usually a 3 or 4 inch black pipe with a cap, roughly level with the landscaping and close to the house. Newer homes sometimes have two; the city says the one nearer the house is the one to use, and it keeps no records of where private cleanouts are. Chandler suggests draining no faster than about 12 gallons per minute, never using a cleanout set in a wall, and shutting the pump off at once if water backs up into the home. If the cleanout is hard to find or the flow seems wrong, the city points people to a plumber. Read more in the guide to how often to drain a pool in Arizona.

Pool fences, power and water savings in Chandler

Chandler's pool barrier rules

Chandler's building safety pages say every pool must be enclosed by a perimeter fence of masonry, concrete or decorative fencing such as wrought iron, and that an existing wood fence has to be replaced when a pool goes in. The fence cannot offer hand or foot holds, and openings must stop a 4 inch ball. Pedestrian gates must close and latch on their own, swing outward from the pool if there is no inner barrier, and have a latch more than 54 inches above the ground. Gates wider than 4 feet must stay locked. A building permit is required before pool fencing is built, and the rules apply whether or not children live in the home. Development Services can answer questions at 480-782-3000.

Whatever the fence, never leave a child alone near a pool, and treat a green or cloudy pool as a hazard because no one can see the bottom. If yours has gone that way, the green pool cleanup page explains what a cleanup involves.

Electricity and gas

Chandler's own economic development material lists both SRP and APS as electric providers in the city, so which one bills you depends on your address. SRP's residential line, (602) 236-8888, answers around the clock for outages. Natural gas comes from Southwest Gas. Neither utility works on pool equipment: when the house has power but the pump sits silent, the fault lies with the pool equipment or the circuit feeding it.

Water conservation rebates

Chandler's single family grass removal rebate paid $1.50 per square foot, up to $2,000, under its current policy, and older city pages listed a higher rate, so confirm the amount with the Water Conservation Office (480-782-3583) before you start. The policy does not reward pools: no pools, fountains or other water-heavy features are allowed in the converted area. The city also offers a rebate of up to $250 on a smart irrigation controller.
