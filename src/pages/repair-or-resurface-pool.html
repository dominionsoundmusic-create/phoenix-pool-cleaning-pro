How often do you resurface a pool? The honest answer is: when the surface says so, not when a calendar does. Two pools built the same year on the same street can be a decade apart in how their finish has aged, mostly because of how their water was kept. This guide is not a how-to. It is a way to decide whether what you see on your pool calls for a spot repair, a full resurface or simply better water care, and what to ask before you spend money on either.

Phoenix Pool Cleaning Pro does not repair or resurface pools, and it does not inspect them. It is a free phone line that connects you with an independent local pool company, which looks at the pool, gives you its own price and does the work. What follows will help you understand what that company tells you.

Start with two questions: how old, and what kind

The age of the current finish and what it is made of set your expectations. HomeGuide puts typical life at about 7 to 12 years for standard plaster, 10 to 15 years for quartz and 15 to 20 years or more for pebble. A finish near or past the end of its span that is showing wear across the pool is a strong candidate for resurfacing. A young finish with one bad spot usually is not.

If you bought the house and do not know the age, look for clues: paperwork from the previous owner, a permit history from your city, or simply the condition of the steps and benches, which tend to wear first. For a closer look at each finish type and the resurfacing process itself, see our pool resurfacing page.

Age and condition questions worth answering

  • How old is the surface, and has it ever been patched before? Many old patches suggest a surface near its end.
  • Where is the damage? One step, one corner or one crack is a local problem. Floor, walls and steps together is a surface problem.
  • Is it getting worse? Take dated photos of the same spots a month or two apart. A spreading problem points one way, a stable one the other.
  • Is the pool losing water faster than evaporation explains? Then the first job is finding a leak, not choosing a finish.
  • What else is due? If the waterline tile, coping or equipment is also near its end, combining work while the pool is empty can make sense.
  • How long will you keep the house? A patch that buys a few good years can be the right call before a sale; a long stay favors a durable finish.

Patches rarely match the color and texture of the surface around them perfectly, especially on an older or faded finish. If looks matter to you, ask to see how a patch will blend before you agree.

Cosmetic cracks versus structural cracks

"Can a cracked pool be repaired?" is one of the most common pool questions, and the answer starts with what kind of crack it is. The finish on a concrete pool is a thin layer over a thick shell. A crack can live only in that thin layer, or it can run through the shell beneath it.

Usually cosmetic

  • Hairline cracks in the finish, sometimes in a web or map pattern
  • Edges flush, with no step from one side to the other
  • No change over weeks of dated photos
  • The water level drops no faster than in nearby pools

These can often be watched, patched or left for the next resurface.

Could be structural

  • The crack is wider than a hairline, or grows over time
  • One side sits higher or lower than the other
  • The pool loses water and the crack is in the wet area
  • Cracks also show in the deck, coping or bond beam near the same spot

Have this inspected before you choose a finish. A new surface over a moving crack will crack again.

If you suspect a leak, our pool leak and crack repair page covers what a leak check involves and what to ask. Do not pour sealants or putties into a crack you have not had diagnosed; they can hide the evidence a technician needs.

How water chemistry shortens surface life

Plaster, quartz and pebble finishes all contain cement. Cement is sensitive to the water touching it every hour of every day, and in a Phoenix backyard that water is hot, sun-blasted and topped up often with hard tap water. Angi's Phoenix resurfacing page names heat, sun, hard water and mineral buildup as local reasons pool interiors wear. Water can go wrong in two opposite directions:

Aggressive water

Water that is low in pH, total alkalinity or calcium hardness is "hungry" for calcium. It pulls calcium out of the cement in the finish, which leaves the surface etched, pitted and rough. Etching often shows first on steps and benches. A fresh fill of soft water, or long stretches of low pH, are common causes.

Scaling water

Water that is high in pH, alkalinity or calcium hardness, and warm, drops calcium out of the water instead. It leaves a rough white or gray crust on the finish and at the waterline. Evaporation takes water out of a pool but leaves its minerals behind, and every top-up adds more, so in the Valley the white crust of scale is a familiar sight.

What the LSI number means

Pool professionals often sum up balance with the Langelier Saturation Index, or LSI. It combines pH, water temperature, calcium hardness (how much dissolved calcium is in the water), total alkalinity and total dissolved solids into one figure. A reading near zero means the water is roughly in balance with the cement in the finish. A negative reading means the water leans aggressive and may etch. A positive reading means it leans toward scale. Because temperature is part of the formula, the same water can drift from one side to the other between a January morning and an August afternoon.

You do not need to calculate it. Ask the company that services your pool whether it checks balance as well as chlorine, and what it does when the readings drift. Never try to correct balance by mixing pool chemicals together: keep products apart, follow the label and leave acid work to a professional.

Weighing the money without guessing

This guide does not give prices; real quotes vary with each pool. What it can give you is a way to compare. Ask the company for two written numbers: the cost of the repair you are considering now, and the cost of resurfacing the pool. Then ask how long the repair is expected to hold and whether more patches are likely soon. A cheap patch on a surface that is failing everywhere can turn into a string of patches. A single resurface on a pool whose surface is otherwise sound can be money spent too early.

What to ask before you decide

  • What exactly is wrong with the surface, and is it local or across the pool?
  • Is any crack structural, and how did you reach that view?
  • Is the pool losing water, and has that been checked?
  • What caused the damage, and what changes in water care would slow it down?
  • How long should a patch last here, and how well will it match?
  • If you recommend resurfacing, which finish and why, and what would you do to the tile and coping while it is empty?
  • What is your Arizona ROC license number and classification?
