What this Phoenix pool line is, and what it is not

A Phoenix pool can go from clear to cloudy in a hot week and from cloudy to green after one dusty storm. When that happens you want one call that reaches a real local company, not a search page full of ads and unreturned messages.

Phoenix Pool Cleaning Pro is meant to be that call for pool cleaning in Phoenix and the towns around it. It is a free phone line plus this website. It is not a pool company: it has no technicians, no trucks, no chemicals and no license, and it never cleans, repairs or prices anything. It answers your call at any hour, asks briefly what is going on with the pool, and transfers you to an independent, locally owned pool service company that works in your area.

That company is the one that looks at the pool, tells you what it found, quotes the work, does it and bills you. Your business is with that company alone. Calling the line costs nothing, and the pool companies may compensate the line for referrals, which the footer of every page discloses.

Why pools in the Valley need more attention

Pool cleaning in Phoenix is not the same job it is in a cooler, wetter city. Three things push the water and the equipment harder here.

Long, extreme heat

The National Weather Service's review of 2024 counted 70 days at or above 110 degrees at Phoenix Sky Harbor, a record, and 143 days at 100 or more. Warm water and strong sun burn through chlorine fast, which is why stabilizer (cyanuric acid) and a steady testing routine matter so much in summer, and why a pool left alone for a week in July can start to turn.

Monsoon dust and storms

The weather service treats June 15 through September 30 as monsoon season. Dust storms coat the water and load the filter, and the storms that follow drop leaves, debris and rain into the pool and can knock out power to the pump. The monsoon pool care guide covers what to do before and after.

Hard water and evaporation

The City of Phoenix says about 58 percent of its drinking water comes from the Salt and Verde rivers through the SRP canal system, about 40 percent from the Colorado River through the Central Arizona Project, and about 2 percent from city wells. Its 2025 water quality report lists total hardness of 172 to 302 parts per million, or about 10 to 17.6 grains per gallon. Every inch that evaporates leaves those minerals behind, and the top-up water adds more. Over time that shows up as a white line of scale on the tile, crusted heater and salt cell parts, and water that eventually needs a partial drain. See pool tile cleaning and how often to drain a pool in Arizona.

Three safety rules for every pool owner

The pool company handles the chemicals and the equipment. These three rules are yours, every day of the year.

Fence the pool off from the house

The CDC says drowning is the leading cause of death for children ages 1 to 4, and that a four-sided isolation fence separating the pool from the house and yard cuts a child's drowning risk by 83 percent compared with a fence around the yard alone. Keep gates self-closing and self-latching, and never leave a child near the water unwatched. A green or murky pool is worse: you cannot see the bottom.

Never mix pool chemicals

The CDC warns never to mix chlorine products with each other, with acid or with anything else, because some combinations release toxic gas. Store each product in its original container, dry, below 95 degrees and locked away from children and pets. In a Phoenix garage in July that takes planning, which is one reason many owners leave chemicals to the pool company.

Gas smell at the heater: get away first

If you smell rotten eggs near a gas pool heater, leave the area, warn others and do not touch the heater, switches or controls. From a safe place, call 911 and Southwest Gas at 877-860-6020, which answers around the clock. Work on gas lines belongs to a licensed professional, never the homeowner.
