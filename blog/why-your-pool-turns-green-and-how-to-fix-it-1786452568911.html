Why Your Pool Turns Green and How to Fix It Fast

You walk out to your backyard on a sunny Phoenix morning, ready for a refreshing swim — and your pool looks like a swamp. That murky, green water is one of the most common (and frustrating) problems pool owners face here in the Valley. The good news? It's fixable. The better news? With the right routine, you can prevent it from ever happening again. This guide breaks down exactly why pools turn green and gives you a clear, step-by-step path to getting your water sparkling clean again.

The Real Reason Your Pool Water Turns Green

Green pool water is almost always caused by algae growth. Algae are microscopic organisms that thrive in warm, unbalanced water — and if you live in Phoenix, you already know we have plenty of heat to go around. When your pool's chlorine levels drop too low, algae seize the opportunity to multiply rapidly, turning your water green seemingly overnight.

Here are the most common triggers:

  • Low chlorine levels — Chlorine is your pool's primary defense against algae. When it drops below 1 ppm, algae can start to take hold within hours.
  • Poor water circulation — Dead spots in your pool where water sits still become breeding grounds for algae.
  • Imbalanced pH levels — Even if you have enough chlorine, a high pH (above 7.8) makes it far less effective.
  • Heavy rain or wind — Storm runoff and debris introduce phosphates and organic material that feed algae.
  • High bather load — Lots of swimmers introduce body oils, sunscreen, and other contaminants that deplete chlorine quickly.
  • Extreme heat — Phoenix summers are brutal. UV rays from the sun rapidly break down unstabilized chlorine, especially in pools without a proper cyanuric acid level.

How to Tell How Bad the Algae Problem Is

Not all green pools are the same. The shade of green and the water's clarity can tell you a lot about how much work you're facing:

  • Light green, slightly cloudy water — Early-stage algae bloom. This is the easiest to treat and can usually be resolved in 24–48 hours with proper shocking and brushing.
  • Medium green, murky water — Moderate algae growth. You may need a double or triple shock treatment and a few days of filtration to clear it up.
  • Dark green or black water — Severe algae infestation. You may not even be able to see the bottom of the pool. This requires aggressive treatment, and in some cases, a partial drain and scrub may be necessary.

Before you start any treatment, test your water. A basic test kit or test strips will give you readings for chlorine, pH, alkalinity, and cyanuric acid — all of which you'll need to address to fix the problem properly.

Step-by-Step: How to Fix a Green Pool

Ready to take action? Here's how to clear up a green pool on your own:

  1. Test your water chemistry. Get baseline readings for pH, chlorine, alkalinity, and cyanuric acid. You'll need this information to know how much of each chemical to add.
  2. Balance your pH first. Aim for a pH between 7.2 and 7.6. If it's too high, add pH decreaser (muriatic acid or sodium bisulfate). Chlorine works much better in this range.
  3. Shock the pool. Use a calcium hypochlorite shock treatment. For a light green pool, use 1 pound per 10,000 gallons. For a dark green pool, triple or quadruple that amount. Shock at dusk to prevent UV degradation.
  4. Brush all surfaces thoroughly. Use a pool brush to scrub the walls, floor, and steps. This dislodges algae and exposes it to the chlorine. Don't skip this step — it dramatically speeds up the process.
  5. Run your filter continuously. Keep your pump and filter running 24 hours a day until the water clears. Clean or backwash your filter every 8–12 hours so it doesn't get clogged and stop working effectively.
  6. Add an algaecide. Once chlorine levels start dropping back down after the shock, add a pool-grade algaecide to kill off any remaining algae spores and prevent regrowth.
  7. Vacuum to waste. After the algae is dead (you'll see it turn gray or white), vacuum the pool on the "waste" setting to remove the dead material without sending it back through your filter.
  8. Retest and balance. Once the water clears, retest all your chemical levels and adjust as needed. Your pool should be back to a safe, swimmable state.

How Long Does It Take to Fix a Green Pool?

The timeline depends on how bad the algae bloom is:

  • Light green: 24–48 hours with proper treatment
  • Medium green: 3–5 days
  • Dark green or black: 5–10 days, sometimes longer

In Phoenix's heat, don't be surprised if algae bounces back quickly if you let your guard down. Consistency is everything. The moment your chlorine drops, algae is ready to come back.

How to Prevent Your Pool From Turning Green Again

Prevention is always easier — and cheaper — than treatment. Here's how to keep your Phoenix pool crystal clear all year long:

  • Test your water 2–3 times per week, especially during summer. Chlorine levels can swing dramatically in our desert heat.
  • Maintain proper chlorine levels between 1–3 ppm at all times. Use stabilized chlorine tablets or add cyanuric acid to protect free chlorine from UV breakdown.
  • Keep your pH between 7.2 and 7.6 consistently. This is the sweet spot for chlorine effectiveness.
  • Run your pool pump at least 8–12 hours per day during summer months. Circulation keeps water moving and harder for algae to settle.
  • Brush your pool walls and floor weekly, even when the water looks clean. This removes early algae growth before it becomes visible.
  • Shock your pool weekly during peak summer and after heavy rain or heavy use.
  • Clean your filter regularly. A clogged filter reduces circulation and water clarity.
